Ten stages, and where your decisions sit

Custom packaging is not one service with one price tag; it is a sequence of about ten handovers, and at each one something has to be decided before the next can start. Some of those decisions are ours — material behaviour, machine order, how a structure is actually built. The rest are yours, and knowing which is which in advance is most of what separates a project that ships on schedule from one that stalls waiting for an answer.

Read the list below as the order things physically happen on our floor, because it is: nothing gets printed before the structure exists, nothing gets assembled before the printed sheets are finished, and nothing gets packed before it has been checked. Exactly this sequence can be watched happening in real time in the film on our factory page.

01

Project Requirements

Product dimensions, weight, quantity, application, artwork, reference images, materials and finishing requirements are reviewed.

What we are doing in this stage is finding the two or three constraints that will actually decide the structure — usually the heaviest part, the most fragile part, and the quantity — rather than collecting every preference you have. Anything that cannot be resolved here becomes a question later, and later costs more to change than now.

02

Structural Development

The packaging structure is developed around product dimensions, opening method, presentation, material thickness, stability, assembly and shipping considerations.

This is the stage where a box either works or does not. Internal clearances are set here, so this is also the last easy moment to tell us the product dimensions are approximate — a structure built against numbers that later change has to be rebuilt, not adjusted.

03

Material Selection

Materials are selected according to the required structure, appearance and application, including greyboard, specialty papers, coated papers, textured papers and other packaging materials.

Material choice carries cost, weight, print behaviour and how the box feels in the hand, and those four rarely pull in the same direction. We will usually put two options in front of you with the trade-off named explicitly rather than pick quietly on your behalf.

04

Quotation

The project is reviewed according to structure, dimensions, materials, quantity, printing, finishing, accessories and shipping requirements.

Quotes are issued on EXW, FOB, CIF or DDP terms, so that what you are comparing is the landed cost rather than a unit price that hides the freight. If a request sits outside what we can make well, this is where we say so.

05

Prototype & Sampling

Samples can be used to confirm dimensions, structure, materials, artwork, printing, finishing, product fit and overall presentation.

This is the only stage designed to be disagreed with. Put your actual product in the sample, ship it somewhere if that is how it will really travel, and tell us what is wrong — a change made here costs a sample, the same change made after printing costs a run.

06

Artwork & Production Specification

Artwork, materials, dimensions, printing, finishing and assembly specifications are finalized before mass production.

Artwork is checked here for bleed, resolution, colour space and panel alignment against the actual cutting shape. Once this document is signed off it becomes the reference the production floor works to and the inspection in stage nine checks against, so it is worth being certain rather than quick.

07

Mass Production

Production can involve board preparation, printing, mounting, die cutting, folding, box forming, magnetic assembly, finishing and interior assembly.

Everything before this point was preparation; this is where the order physically exists. Work passes between lines rather than staying at one station, which is why tooling is checked against a first-off piece before the run continues.

08

Printing & Finishing

Finishing options may include foil stamping, embossing, debossing, spot UV, lamination and specialty papers.

Every finish is another pass through another machine, and each pass carries its own registration tolerance. Two finishes register cleanly; four take real setup discipline, and we will tell you when a combination is asking for more than the artwork gains from it.

09

Quality Control

Production checks are used to verify important packaging specifications before shipment.

Checking at several points during a run rather than only at the end is what keeps a sixteenth Box one hundred problem from being a sixteen hundred box one. What exactly gets checked, and against which reference, is set out on the quality control page.

10

Packing & Shipment

Finished packaging is packed according to agreed requirements and prepared for shipment.

Carton pack-out, labelling, palletising and moisture protection are decided against how the goods actually travel — sea freight and courier parcels ask for different things, and a factory box that survives a pallet can still lose a corner to a distribution centre.

How long the whole sequence takes

Two ranges cover almost every project, and both start from a fixed specification rather than from the day you asked: samples take 5 – 8 working days once the specification and artwork are settled, and production runs 12 – 20 working days from the later of the cleared deposit or your written sample approval.

Which end of each range you land on depends on how many printing passes the artwork needs, whether an insert has to be tooled, how much of the assembly is done by hand, and the size of the run. Those are all known at quotation time, so the honest answer to "how long will this take" has already been worked out before you commit to anything.

Sample parcels go out with DHL, UPS or FedEx, typically 3 – 6 days in transit, and production can ship on EXW, FOB, CIF or DDP terms depending on who you want arranging the freight.
