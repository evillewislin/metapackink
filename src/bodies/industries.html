What actually changes between sectors

Every project starts from the same four facts: what the product is, how large and heavy it is, how many pieces you need, and where the finished packaging has to arrive. What differs from one sector to the next is which of those four bites hardest, what the box has to survive in between, and how much of your margin is allowed to sit in the packaging.

A cosmetic box that has to survive a courier network is not the same problem as a fragrance box that has to hold a glass bottle dead centre, and neither is a retail shipper whose real audience is a distribution centre rather than a shelf. We build all of these, and the sections below set out what genuinely changes in each case rather than one description wearing five different product names.

Six things that actually move the unit cost

Packaging quotes are opaque mainly because nobody explains which inputs moved them. These are the six that matter most on almost every quoting round we run, in roughly the order they move the number.

  • Quantity. Printing plates, cutting dies and machine make-ready cost roughly the same whether the run is 500 or 5,000. Spreading them over more pieces is the single largest source of unit-cost movement.
  • Board and paper. Greyboard thickness and the choice of wrap paper set both the cost and how the box feels when it is lifted. A 2 mm difference across a large run is real money and real presence.
  • How many printing passes. Each foil colour, each emboss and each spot UV pass is its own trip through a machine with its own registration risk. Three finishes on one box is not one finish multiplied by three.
  • Minutes of hand work. Any step a person performs by hand is priced per piece and does not get cheaper with volume in the same way machine time does. This is where presentation boxes get expensive quietly.
  • Inserts and interior structure. A flat die-cut card divider and a moulded pulp or foam cradle solve the same problem at very different prices, and the right answer depends on how the product has to arrive.
  • Packing and logistics. Individual polybagging, retail-ready cartons, palletisation requirements and chosen Incoterms all land in the landed cost rather than the unit cost — which is why two quotes for the same box can differ and both be honest.

None of these are surcharges invented after the fact. If you tell us which two or three matter most to you at the start, we will quote the trade-off openly rather than quote one option as though it were the only one.

Samples, lead times and shipping terms

A physical sample is produced before every production run, and it becomes the reference the whole order is measured against — the same piece you can see being built in the two-minute film on our about page.

Once the specification and artwork are fixed, samples take 5 – 8 working days. Mass production then runs 12 – 20 working days from the later of the cleared deposit or your written sample approval. Sample parcels travel by DHL, UPS or FedEx, which is typically 3 – 6 days in transit, and quotations can be issued on EXW, FOB, CIF or DDP terms so that you are comparing like with like against your existing supplier.

What those ranges depend on is honest to say in advance: how many printing passes the artwork needs, whether an insert has to be tooled, and how large the run is. We will tell you which side of each range your project sits on when we quote rather than after you have committed.
