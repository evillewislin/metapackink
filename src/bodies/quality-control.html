Quality control is a reference, not an opinion

Nothing can be "good" or "not good" until someone has written down what good means for this specific box. Our quality control starts from an approved physical sample and a signed-off specification, and every check on this page is a comparison against one of those two things — not a judgement made on the day by whoever happens to be looking.

That distinction matters more than any equipment list. A factory that checks "carefully" but against nothing in particular will hand you a run that looked fine when it left and argues with you when it arrives. A factory checking against a document you both signed will tell you, before shipment, exactly which measurement is out and by how much.

The ten stages these checks sit inside are set out on the manufacturing process page; what happens at each inspection point is described below.

Where the checks happen

Checks are spread across the run rather than stacked at the end, because a problem found after sixteen hundred boxes are finished is sixteen hundred boxes of problem, while the same problem found at first-off is one.

Not every project needs every line at full intensity, and we will not pad an inspection to make it look thorough. What we will do is tell you which checks apply to your structure and what each one is looking for.

What We Check

Dimensions can be checked against approved specifications. Materials can be checked against the agreed packaging specification. Printing can be reviewed for artwork consistency, color reproduction, printing quality and registration. Finishing can be checked for foil, embossing, debossing, spot UV, lamination and surface appearance.

Structure & Assembly

Structural and assembly checks help verify that packaging is constructed according to the approved design. Depending on the project, this may include box construction, folding, magnetic closure, drawer movement, lid fit, interior assembly and product positioning.

Quality Control During Production

Quality control is not limited to final inspection. Depending on the project, checks can take place during different production stages so that issues can be identified before final shipment.

Sample Approval

Approved samples provide an important reference for production. Before mass production, packaging structure, materials, artwork and finishing can be reviewed and confirmed.

Final Inspection

Before shipment, finished packaging can be checked against agreed project requirements. Final inspection may cover quantity, dimensions, materials, printing, finishing, structure, assembly and overall appearance.

Quality Starts With Clear Specifications

A clear packaging specification helps reduce misunderstandings. Recommended information includes product dimensions, box dimensions, materials, board thickness, printing, finishing, structure, interior requirements, quantity and packing requirements.

What goes into a specification we can actually check

Most quality disputes are not really about quality — they are about a specification that left something unstated and two people who reasonably assumed different things. The list below is what we ask for, and each line is there because its absence has caused a disagreement somewhere before.

  • Product dimensions and weight, including any part that overhangs the body of the product.
  • Finished box dimensions and, where it matters, internal clearance.
  • Materials — board thickness and grade, wrap paper, and insert material.
  • Printing — colours, how many passes, and which reference the colour is matched to.
  • Finishing — foil, emboss, deboss, spot UV, lamination, soft-touch, and where each sits.
  • Structure and interior — opening method, magnets, ribbon, dividers, cradles and how the product is held.
  • Tolerance where you have one. If you do not, say so and we will tell you what is normal for that structure rather than leave it implied.
  • Quantity and packing — pieces per carton, labelling, palletising and any retailer requirement.

Send this as a document rather than a conversation. Anything written down can be checked; anything agreed verbally has to be remembered, and memory is the least reliable inspection tool there is.

What inspection does, and what it cannot do

Saying this plainly saves more trouble than promising more than we can deliver: inspection is a sampling activity, not a guarantee that every single piece is perfect. Its purpose is to find a problem while the run is still here and can still be corrected, instead of arguing about it after the goods have landed four thousand kilometres away.

Three things follow from that. First, the earlier a check happens the more it is worth, which is why checks sit inside the run rather than at the end of it. Second, the sharper your specification is, the more of your own standard we can hold the run to — a vague specification produces a vague result whatever the inspection looks like. Third, for larger orders many buyers appoint their own inspector before shipment; we work to whatever written standard you name and would rather have a named one than an implied one.

What inspection cannot do is fix a decision made upstream. A structure that was never quite right for the product, or artwork supplied too late to be checked properly, will produce a run that passes every check on this page and still disappoints. That is what stages one to six of the process exist to prevent.
