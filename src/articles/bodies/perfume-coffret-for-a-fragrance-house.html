The brief was a coffret for a 100 ml fragrance, sold through the brand's own retail and shipped direct to customers as well. One bottle, one box, no secondary components. It is the simplest kind of perfume packaging there is, and it is worth writing up because the whole structure turned on a single decision about where the bottle's weight goes — a decision that is normally made by default rather than made at all.

Client name, artwork and commercial figures are left out. Dimensions, board, wrap, insert construction, clearances and quantity band are not, because those are the parts that transfer to another project.

The bottle being boxed

  • Capacity: 100 ml, filled weight approximately 430 g including the cap
  • Footprint: 55 × 55 mm, square with radiused corners
  • Height: 135 mm including the cap; the cap itself is 28 mm across and 32 mm tall
  • Shoulder: 48 mm across at the height where the glass turns in toward the neck

So: a heavy, hard, brittle object with a narrow neck and a protruding cap, in a box that has to be opened by a customer and survive a courier network. Every decision below follows from taking that description literally rather than treating the bottle as a rectangle.

The decision: the load goes through the foot

The standard perfume insert is a block of foam or EVA with a silhouette cut into it. The bottle lies into the cavity and the cavity grips it along its whole length. It works. It was rejected here for two reasons: the brand did not want foam, and a silhouette cavity grips glass hard enough that lifting the bottle out is a two-hand operation — which is the opposite of the unboxing a fragrance wants.

That left paper-based options, and it forced the question that foam lets you avoid: which part of the bottle actually carries its weight?

The answer is the foot. A glass bottle is strongest in compression through its base, where the glass is thickest, and weakest at the neck, where the wall is thin and the thread concentrates stress. So the specification was written the way a structure should be: the foot of the bottle rests on the base panel of the box and carries the load. Everything above the foot only locates.

Everything else in the insert follows from that sentence. The shoulder shelf is not load-bearing, so it can be made from 1.5 mm board and the whole insert stays light. The collar at the base stops the foot sliding. The clearance above the cap is guaranteed, because a bottle resting on its own foot cannot rise.

The shelf, and the one dimension it turns on

The shelf is a horizontal panel of die-cut board with an aperture the neck passes through. It does two jobs: it stops the bottle tipping sideways, and it stops it rising.

The aperture diameter is the whole design. It has to be larger than the cap so the bottle can be dropped in from above, and smaller than the shoulder so the shoulder actually lands on something:

  • Cap: 28 mm across
  • Aperture: 36 mm — 8 mm over the cap, so the bottle drops in without catching, and 12 mm under the 48 mm shoulder diameter, so the shoulder meets board rather than passing through it
  • Tolerance: the shoulder is a slope, not a step, so the contact is a ring rather than a line. That is useful: it makes the fit tolerant of small variations in the glass from batch to batch

The shelf stands on four folded legs that land near the corners of the base panel, where the box is stiffest, rather than in the middle where a load would bow it. The legs are folded from the same piece of board as the shelf — no separate parts, no adhesive, one fold per leg.

The cap that lives in the lid

The base is 110 mm deep on the inside. The bottle is 135 mm tall. That leaves 25 mm of the bottle — the cap and part of the neck — standing proud of the base rim, inside the lid.

That is a deliberate choice rather than a compromise. Making the base deep enough to swallow the whole bottle would have added 25 mm of exterior height for no benefit and made the bottle harder to lift out. Letting the cap occupy the lid instead means the lid only has to clear what is actually up there.

The clearance that matters is the gap between the top of the cap and the inside face of the lid. It was set at 5 mm, and the reason it is 5 mm rather than 2 mm is recorded below.

Structure and specification

  • Structure: two-piece rigid box, base interior 70 × 70 × 110 mm, lid with a 30 mm skirt
  • Board: 2.5 mm greyboard in the base, 2.0 mm in the lid. The base carries 430 g of glass and the pallet stack above it; the lid only has to hold its shape and close flat, so putting the extra half-millimetre in the lid would have added weight to the part the customer lifts without stiffening anything that needed it.
  • Wrap: 157 gsm coated paper with a light linen texture, matte lamination, foil stamped on the front panel only. Foil was kept off the textured areas — adhesion on this stock was tested at sampling rather than assumed.
  • Insert: die-cut 1.5 mm greyboard, one piece — a shoulder shelf on four folded legs, with a separate 12 mm collar at the base locating the foot. No tooling beyond a cutting die, no adhesive, no foam.
  • Clearances: 36 mm shelf aperture; 5 mm between the top of the cap and the inside face of the lid.
  • Interior: single-colour print on the lid's interior face. The plate is the only real cost, so once the wrap is being printed anyway this is close to free, and on a two-piece box the inside of the lid is the first thing the customer sees.
  • Quantity: low thousands, in two colourways that differ only in the wrap and the foil.

What went wrong, and what it cost

One thing, and it is worth recording because it is invisible on a drawing.

The first sample's shelf was cut to the shoulder height with no allowance, which left the foot of the bottle floating roughly a millimetre above the base panel. Nothing about the sample looked wrong: the bottle sat level, the shelf held it, the lid closed. But the load path was the one we had specified against — the bottle was hanging from its shoulder rather than standing on its foot, and every gram of 430 was going through the neck.

In the drop test, one bottle cracked at the shoulder. That is the failure mode this specification exists to prevent, and it happened because the specification was written correctly and then built a millimetre wrong.

The fix was to raise the shelf by 1.5 mm so the foot lands before the shoulder can touch. The second sample passed the same test.

A second change came out of the same round. The cap clearance had been set at 2 mm, which is enough at rest and not enough under a shock: the lid deflects downward on impact, and the cap marked the interior of the lid on one of the drops. It went to 5 mm, with a board stiffener under the lid panel rather than a deeper lid — the stiffener holds the deflection down, which is the actual cause, rather than simply moving the lid further away.

What to take from it

  • Decide where the load goes before designing the insert. Through the foot in compression, not through the shoulder or the neck. Say it in writing, then check on the sample that the part you nominated is actually touching.
  • A shelf aperture has two limits, not one. Bigger than the cap so the bottle goes in; smaller than the shoulder so it stays in. Both are knowable from the bottle drawing before anything is cut.
  • Let the cap occupy the lid rather than deepening the base to hide it. The exterior height you do not add is freight you do not pay, twice over on a pallet.
  • Set clearance for the deflected lid, not the resting one. Two millimetres is a static measurement; a drop is not static. If clearance is tight, stiffen the panel instead of deepening the box.
  • Split the board specification between base and lid when the two halves do different work. The base carries the stack; the lid carries nothing but its own shape.

The finished coffret met the brief and the structure has held through repeat runs. The reusable part is smaller than the project: on glass, the question is never how heavy the bottle is. It is which part of it you have chosen to hold.
