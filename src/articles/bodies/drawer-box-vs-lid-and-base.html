Ask for a rigid box and you will usually be shown three structures before you are shown a price: a lid that lifts off a base, a lid hinged to the base that closes on magnets, and a tray that slides out of a sleeve. The first two get compared constantly. The third is normally chosen on feel — somebody has seen one in a watch shop — and then discovered halfway through tooling that a drawer box is not the same box with a different opening. It is a box with a tolerance in it.

That tolerance is the whole subject. A lid-and-base box has two parts that have to fit once, at the moment the lid is put on. A drawer box has two parts that have to fit every single time, in every orientation, after a long journey, and the fit cannot be tight. Everything else — the insert, the pull, the assembly sequence, the failure modes on a packing line — follows from that one sentence.

The mechanism, and the tolerance it introduces

A lid-and-base box is two wrapped trays. The lid is cut slightly larger than the base and slides down over its skirt, and it is held by the friction between two wrapped faces. The parts meet once and then stay where they are.

A drawer box is a tray inside a sleeve. The sleeve is a rectangular tube — four walls, no top and no floor — and the tray slides along its length. Nothing holds the tray in and nothing stops it, so the sleeve's interior has to be larger than the tray's exterior on every face, by a gap that has to survive being handled, shipped and opened.

Three things follow immediately, and they are the ones that catch projects out:

  • The tray is never centred. There is no register between the two parts, so a drawer box has no defined closed position unless something stops it. A lid-and-base box does: the lid bottoms out on the base's rim.
  • The gap has to exist after the board has moved. Paperboard takes up moisture and swells, and it swells more across the grain than with it. A clearance that is right in the factory can be gone after a sea freight leg.
  • The gap is what makes the box feel cheap when it is wrong in either direction. Too tight and the tray fights; too loose and the tray rattles, and a rattle in a rigid box reads as a defect even when nothing is broken.

Where the sliding clearance has to come from

There are only two places the clearance can come from: you shrink the tray, or you grow the sleeve. They are not equivalent, and choosing the wrong one is the most common drawer box specification error.

Shrinking the tray takes the dimension out of the one part that cannot afford to give. The tray's exterior sets its interior, the interior sets the cavity, and the cavity sets the fitment that holds the product. Take a millimetre off the tray so it slides, and you have taken a millimetre off the product's grip — the customer opens the box to find the item sitting at the back of its own tray.

Growing the sleeve leaves the tray, the cavity and the fitment at the size they need to be, and costs exterior volume instead. On a box under about 200 mm that volume is worth less than the grip is, and it is the direction we specify every time.

The working figure is 0.5 mm to 0.8 mm per side, in both width and height. Below half a millimetre the board's own moisture movement closes the gap; above about one millimetre the tray visibly rocks. It is worth saying why the top and bottom of the range are not interchangeable: a small tray in a dry climate can run at the tight end, and anything crossing an ocean should not.

The other thing to know about that number is that it is not the number that fails. The defect that jams a drawer is a sleeve whose corners were formed slightly proud, so the interior measures correct across the middle of each panel and tight at the four corners — which is exactly where the tray's corners travel. A ruler across the panel will not find it, because the panel is right.

The pull: ribbon, notch, and the load path

A tray needs something to pull, and the three usual answers have very different failure modes.

A ribbon. The best-looking option and the one most likely to fail. Glued to the outer face of the tray, the pull load runs through the adhesive bond into a coated or laminated paper surface, and it releases — often at well under the force a customer applies when a drawer sticks. The fix is not a stronger adhesive but a different load path: a slot is cut through the tray's front board, the ribbon end is passed through it and trapped under the wrap, so the load goes into the board. The failure then moves from the glue line to the ribbon itself, which is where you want it.

A thumb notch cut into the sleeve. Cheap, reliable, and no bond to fail, because the load goes into the sleeve rather than through anything. It costs you a hole in the front of the box: dust gets in, the tray front is visible through it, and the opening reads cheaper than a ribbon.

A finger hole in the tray's front panel. Works well and hides nothing, but it needs a tray front of some depth to cut into, so it pushes the tray dimensions.

Whichever is used, the pull load is small — a few hundred grams — and that is the trap. It is applied to the only part of the box that moves, every time the box is opened, and on a run of a few thousand it is applied a few thousand times more often than any other load on the box.

What the drawer does to the insert

A drawer converts a pull on the ribbon into movement of everything inside it. That only works if the fitment grips the cavity harder than the tray resists the sleeve. If the two are close, the tray comes out and the product stays where it was.

In practice that means the fitment is specified as an interference fit rather than a friction one: a cavity cut slightly undersize so a resilient material compresses to seat, and holds through compression rather than through surface grip. Compression does not change as the covering fabric wears, which is the reason a friction fit that is perfect on day one is loose by the time the box reaches a customer.

The drawer also takes away a volume a lid-and-base box gives you for free. On a two-piece box the lid's interior is usable space — a tall cap can sit inside it, which is how a tall bottle is boxed without adding height to the base. A drawer box has no equivalent, so a tall product has to be laid down or the tray has to be deeper, and a deeper tray means a taller sleeve and more exterior volume in the one direction freight notices.

Assembly and packing time

A lid-and-base box packs as a pile. Bases are lined up, inserts dropped in, products seated, and lids put on at the end. Part-assembled boxes can be stacked and moved between stations, and the whole sequence tolerates being interrupted.

A drawer box packs as a sequence. The insert goes into the tray, the product goes into the insert, and the tray then has to go into the sleeve before the unit can be stacked at all — because a tray that is not inside its sleeve has nothing to keep its corners square and cannot be piled. So the line needs the sleeves at the same station rather than at the end, and it cannot build up a buffer of part-packed units.

Add the pull — ribbon threaded and dressed, or a notch checked — and the drawer is realistically one to two operations more per unit. On a few hundred boxes that is nothing. On a run where packing labour is the constraint, it is the difference between a line that finishes in a day and one that finishes in a day and a half.

What each one does on the way to the customer

This is the drawer's real weakness and it should be stated plainly rather than discovered. A friction-held lid walks off a lid-and-base box under vibration; a drawer extends. It does not need much encouragement — repeated low-level shock in one direction is enough to walk a tray out of a sleeve by ten or fifteen millimetres, and a tray standing that far proud of the carton is what gets crushed at the carton edge, or opens inside the parcel and empties.

The fixes are all real and all cost something:

  • A tighter fit — which is the wrong answer, because it is the thing that makes the drawer fight the customer.
  • A stop. A sleeve closed at one end stops the tray going backwards. Nothing in the structure stops it coming forwards, which is the direction it travels.
  • A paper band or belly band around the sleeve, which is the cheapest honest fix and is usually the one we recommend for a drawer that ships direct.
  • An outer shipper. Correct, and it is what a rigid box should have anyway — a rigid box is not a shipping container.

What the drawer gains in return is real too: the tray cannot be lost, the box opens one-handed on a table, and the contents are presented from above in a way a lift-off lid interrupts. That is why the structure keeps being asked for.

Exterior volume, and what the sleeve actually costs

There is a persistent belief that a drawer box is the heavier, bulkier option. For the same interior it is usually the opposite, and it is worth understanding why, because it changes what the two structures should be compared on.

A lid-and-base box is two trays: a base with a floor and four walls, and a lid with a top panel and four walls. A drawer box is a tray with a floor and four walls, plus a sleeve of four walls with no top and no floor. The sleeve is cheaper in board and in wrap area than the lid it replaces.

What the drawer spends instead is tolerance and attention. The sleeve has to be formed as a tube whose interior is consistent at the corners, and that is a hand operation with a tighter inspection criterion than anything on a lid. The yield effect follows: a sleeve with proud corners is scrap, and it is discovered at the end of assembly rather than at the start, so the reject costs the whole box.

Where the drawer does lose on volume is in one specific way. The tray needs to be gripped, so either a ribbon hangs out of the front or the sleeve has a notch — and a tray that is fully buried in its sleeve with no pull is a tray nobody can open. Budget for that, not for the walls.

Where the cost difference comes from

Not the board, which is less. The difference is in the operations: forming the sleeve as a tube to a tolerance, sizing the tray to slide against it, fitting the pull, and a slide check on every unit rather than on a sample.

In our experience that puts a drawer box somewhere in the region of ten to thirty per cent above an equivalent lid-and-base box at the same size, board and finish, with the spread mostly driven by which pull is specified and by how much inspection the slide needs. Unlike a magnetic closure the difference does scale down reasonably at low quantity, because there are no bought components with a minimum order — it is labour, and labour is priced per operation whether the run is five hundred or five thousand.

When each one is the wrong answer

A drawer box is wrong when the box ships direct with no outer and no band; when the product is heavy enough to press the tray against the sleeve floor under its own weight, because that converts a slide into a binding drawer at exactly the moment the customer pulls; when the product is tall and needs the volume a lid interior would have given you; when packing labour is the binding constraint; and when the reveal you are designing is a lid coming away in one movement, which a drawer cannot do.

A lid-and-base box is wrong in the mirror image of those cases: when the box travels on its own so the lid can walk off, when the customer will carry it by the base with the lid doing nothing but friction, when there is nowhere to put the lid down, and when the box will be opened repeatedly after delivery — a lift-off lid is two-handed every time.

The checks, in order

  • Take the slide clearance out of the sleeve, never out of the tray. The tray's dimensions are what hold the product; the sleeve's are what ship.
  • Approve the sample loaded, not empty. A tray that glides with nothing in it can bind with the product's weight pressing it onto the sleeve floor.
  • Gauge the sleeve interior rather than measuring it. A go/no-go gauge cut to the minimum interior dimension finds proud corners in a second; a calliper across a panel will not, because the panel is correct.
  • Pull the ribbon hard, with the box held by the sleeve. The ribbon should fail before its anchorage does. If the bond lets go first, the load path is wrong and no adhesive will fix it.
  • Drop the packed box on the sleeve's open end. This is the test that finds a tray that extends, and it is the one nobody runs on a sample.
  • Specify a matte lamination on the sleeve's inner face. It seals the board against moisture uptake, which is what closes the clearance after a sea leg, and it lowers the sliding friction at the same time.
  • If the box ships direct, add a band. Nothing in the structure stops a tray travelling forwards, and a paper band is cheaper than a return.

None of this requires a buyer to become a packaging engineer. It requires knowing that a drawer box is a box with a gap in it, that the gap has to come from the sleeve, and that the two things worth checking on a sample are the two a photograph cannot show: how it slides when it is full, and whether it stays in when it is dropped.
