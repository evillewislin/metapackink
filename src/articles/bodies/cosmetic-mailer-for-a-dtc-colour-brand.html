The brief was a direct-to-consumer mailer for a colour cosmetics brand launching two products together — a pressed powder palette and a liquid foundation. It had to arrive unbroken through a courier network, look like the brand on a doorstep, and be opened by a customer who had already decided to film it.

It is worth writing up because the brief contained a contradiction that most DTC packaging briefs contain and few state openly: this one box had to be a shipping container and a presentation box at the same time, and those two jobs want opposite things. Naming which one loses — and saying so to the client before anything was cut — is most of what made the project work.

The two products being boxed

  • A pressed powder palette, 118 × 78 × 15 mm closed, 165 g, with a glass mirror bonded into the inside of its lid
  • A 30 ml liquid foundation in a glass bottle with a screw pump, 108 mm tall including the pump head, 145 g filled
  • A folded card, 100 × 140 mm, carrying the shade name and the how-to

Around 330 g in total, in a box that ended up at 212 × 152 × 58 mm. Two fragile things and one leaky one, and a customer who opens the parcel before they open the product.

The contradiction in the brief

A shipping container wants a tight cavity with no voids, a material that absorbs impact, a closure that cannot open by accident, and a print finish nobody cares about.

A presentation box wants the product visible the instant the lid comes up, a surface worth printing on, a closure that opens easily, and materials that read as considered rather than protective.

You cannot have all eight. What you can do is decide which four you are giving up, write it down, and design around it. Here the compromise was stated in the first conversation: the exterior would be specified for survival and the interior would be specified for the photograph. Everything after that followed from it.

E-flute, and solving crush structurally rather than by upgrading the board

The first question on any mailer is the flute. E-flute is about 1.5 mm thick, prints almost like a carton and folds sharply, which is why printed mailers are usually made from it. B-flute is roughly twice that, resists crushing far better and cushions more — and it shows the flute lines through any solid area of print, which on a brand colour panel is visible from arm's length.

The obvious move when a drop test fails is to go up a flute. It was the wrong move here for two reasons: the flute shadow would have run through the brand's solid panels, and 3 mm of extra board on every face is exterior volume, which at this quantity is paid for twice — once in the carton and once in the parcel.

So the board stayed at E-flute, single wall with a 250 gsm white kraft liner, and the crush problem was solved in the fitment instead. That is the better order generally: buy the print surface you want and make the interior carry the load, rather than buying protection with board thickness and then trying to print on it.

One die-cut piece doing three jobs

The fitment is a single 350 gsm paperboard blank, folded into a tray with two wells and a divider. It is doing three separate things and each one was a decision.

It holds the palette lid-down. This is the one worth arguing for. The obvious presentation is lid-up — you open the box and see the brand embossed on the compact lid. It is also the orientation that breaks the mirror. A compact mirror is bonded at its edges and unsupported across the middle, so a load on the lid flexes it, and glass flexed across an unsupported span cracks. Shipped lid-down, the mirror rests flat on the tray floor and is supported across its whole area, and the load path on a drop runs from the tray floor through the compact's base rather than through its lid. The fix is free and it is a drawing change, not a material change.

It fixes a second problem at the same time. A compact lid is held shut by a friction catch, and vibration is very good at opening friction catches. Lid-down, any powder that escapes stays inside a compact that is being held closed by the tray floor, rather than dusting the interior of a box the customer is about to film.

It locates the bottle and takes top-load off the pump. The bottle's foot rests on the tray floor, which is supported by the mailer base; the well only locates it laterally. Nothing is asked to carry the bottle by its neck. Above the pump head sits a paperboard collar — a folded sleeve around the bottle's shoulder, taller than the pump by about 3 mm — so that any downward load on the top panel lands on the collar and goes into the shoulder, not into the pump.

It keeps the glass away from the mirror. The collar doubles as the divider between the two wells, which is why one component does all three jobs. Without it, the bottle's travel under impact is towards the only other hard object in the box.

What went wrong, and what it cost

The first fitment was EVA foam, and it was rejected. It protected the products perfectly and failed on everything else: the unboxing read as industrial, and the brand's own claim was kerbside recyclable, which a foam block contradicts in the customer's hands rather than in a specification. Replacing it with paperboard gave back real protection, and that protection then had to be recovered by geometry — the lid-down orientation and the shoulder collar — rather than by material. That is the honest trade: paperboard protects less per millimetre, so with paperboard the fitment has to be the thing that is engineered.

The mirror cracked before the orientation was changed. Two units out of five in the first drop test, a craze pattern across the mirror rather than a clean break, which is what an unsupported span loaded in the middle produces. Nothing else in the box was damaged. It was the clearest result in the whole project and it cost one redraw.

Four millimetres of clearance above the pump was not enough. The first sample relied on clearance alone, and it held on the bench. It leaked on one unit in the compression test, because clearance only works while the top panel stays where it is — and a courier stack pushes the top panel down. The collar is what actually stops the pump being actuated, because it removes the load instead of giving it somewhere to go.

The tuck flap opened in transit. A standard friction tuck, correct on the drawing, popped under roughly 2 kg of top-load in the carton test and again on a corner drop. The fix was a paper seal — a self-adhesive brand label across the seam, printed with an open-here mark. It carries no load in normal handling, it is the difference between a box that stays shut on a belt sorter and one that does not, and it doubles as the tamper evidence a customer photographs before opening. It costs a few cents and it solved a failure the structure could not.

Paying for the inside instead of the outside

The exterior is CMYK with a matte lamination and nothing else. No foil: a courier belt abrades foil, and abraded foil reads as damage rather than as luxury. The lamination is there for scuff and for the moisture of a doorstep, not for the way it looks.

The interior carries a single-colour flood and a one-colour repeat pattern. On a mailer this is where the money belongs, because the interior is the unboxing surface and it is the only surface in the box that nothing rubs against.

One placement detail is worth recording. This box is the parcel, so a carrier label is going to be slapped across its largest face by somebody who has not seen the artwork. The brand mark was moved off the centre of that face for exactly that reason. A design that assumes the label lands somewhere sensible will be wrong on a meaningful share of parcels, and it is a five-minute change before printing and an unfixable one after.

Structure and specification

  • Structure: one-piece E-flute mailer, tuck-in front flap with a rear dust flap, closed with a paper seal
  • Board: single-wall E-flute, approximately 1.5 mm, 250 gsm white kraft liner. Chosen for print quality first; the crush requirement was met by the fitment rather than by moving to B-flute.
  • Finished size: 212 × 152 × 58 mm
  • Fitment: one die-cut 350 gsm paperboard blank folded into a two-well tray — a shallow well holding the palette lid-down, a deeper well locating the bottle, and a folded collar over the bottle shoulder acting as both divider and top-load path
  • Print: exterior CMYK plus matte lamination, no foil; interior one-colour flood plus a one-colour pattern
  • Closure: friction tuck plus a self-adhesive paper seal across the seam
  • Quantity: low thousands per drop, repeated across the year

What the rigid box would have cost

A two-piece rigid box inside a corrugated shipper was quoted alongside it, and it came back at roughly three times the unit cost, with enough extra weight per parcel to move the shipping band.

We recommended against it, which is not an argument a supplier makes often and is worth being honest about the reasoning for. The rigid version is a better object. It is also a box the customer opens once, films, and then keeps on a shelf for a year without opening again — and at this price point and this shipping profile, the extra cost buys a presentation that the product's margin does not support.

The mailer is the right answer for a DTC launch that repeats. It would be the wrong answer for the same two products in a department store, where the box is handled, opened and re-closed by strangers and where the job the packaging does is different.

What to take from it

  • Say which job loses before anything is drawn. One box cannot be an optimum shipping container and an optimum presentation box. Naming the compromise first is what stops it being discovered at the drop test.
  • Solve crush with the fitment, not with a thicker flute. Board thickness costs exterior volume and print quality; geometry costs a redraw.
  • Ship a compact lid-down. The mirror is supported across its whole face instead of being flexed across an unsupported span, and the friction catch is held closed by the tray floor. It is free.
  • Never rely on clearance above a pump. Clearance assumes the top panel stays put, and a stack pushes it down. A collar over the shoulder removes the load instead.
  • Put the finishing budget on the interior. The exterior of a mailer gets abraded; the interior is the surface the customer photographs.
  • Assume the carrier label lands on the middle of your largest panel. Place the brand mark somewhere that survives it.
  • Add a paper seal to a friction tuck. It is the cheapest fix for the failure a mailer actually has, and customers read it as tamper evidence rather than as tape.

The box shipped, the drop test passed and the return rate on breakages sat where the brand needed it to. The reusable part is smaller than that: the two hard problems in this project — a cracked mirror and a leaking pump — were both solved by changing where a load goes, and neither was solved by adding material. That is usually true of a mailer, and it is the reason the fitment is the part worth spending the design time on.
