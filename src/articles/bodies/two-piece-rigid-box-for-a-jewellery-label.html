The brief was one box for a jewellery range. Not one box per product — one box, one print, one structure, holding seven SKUs that differed in every dimension that usually decides a box: four rings across a size run, and three pendants of quite different heights.

It is a common brief and it is almost always answered by sizing the box to the largest item and hoping. That answer produces a box in which the smallest item rattles, which is worse than a box that is slightly too big. This project is worth writing up because the fix was not a compromise: a single slit, cut to a length that no ring in the range can pass, holds every ring in the range with the same grip whether the band is 2 mm or 6 mm wide.

Names, artwork and commercial figures are left out. Dimensions, board, wrap, insert material, finishing and quantity band are not, because those are the parts a buyer with a similar range can reuse.

The range being boxed

  • Four rings, UK sizes J to R. Band outer diameter 16.8 mm at the small end to 20.6 mm at the large end. Band width from 2.0 mm on a solitaire to 6.0 mm on a wide band. The stone setting stands up to 8 mm above the band on two of the four.
  • Three pendants, 12 mm to 30 mm tall, each on a 420 mm chain.

Roughly 3,500 boxes in total, about 500 per SKU. Two things about that list drove everything. The first is that the rings differ in outer diameter by nearly 4 mm, which is a large fraction of a small box. The second is that a ring is not a rigid object in a box: it stands on its band, it can rotate in whatever holds it, and the part that gets damaged is the setting at the top.

The obvious answer, and why it was wrong

The first proposal was the standard one — a die-cut paperboard insert with a slot per SKU, or a moulded insert with a cavity per SKU, and a box sized to the largest. Both fail the same way, and the failure is not the one people expect.

A cavity that fits the largest ring does not hold the smallest one. It locates it, which is a different thing: the small ring sits in a space wider than itself, and the box then has to survive a courier network with a ring free to move inside a cavity. On a ring with an 8 mm setting, that movement ends with the setting touching something. Two of the five units in the first drop test came out with a marked stone.

Cutting a cavity per SKU solves it and costs seven tools, seven sets of drawings, and a packing line that has to pick the right insert for the right ring — which on a run of 500 per SKU is where mis-packs come from.

The second proposal — a foam block with a slit, sized from the largest ring — was closer and failed more instructively, which is why it is described below.

The decision: a slit that wedges, not a cavity that fits

A ring pushed band-first into a slit in a resilient pad is not held by the slit's faces. It is held by the slit's ends.

The geometry is worth spelling out because it is the reusable part. Take a ring of outer diameter D pushed down into a slit of length L. The width of the ring where it crosses the pad's surface grows as it goes deeper, reaches its maximum at half the diameter, then shrinks again. So the ring wedges at the depth where its width equals L — and it can only do that if L is shorter than D.

That gives two things a cavity cannot:

  • One slit holds a range. A larger ring wedges at a shallower depth, a smaller one deeper. The wedge finds its own depth; nobody has to specify seven of them.
  • Grip does not depend on band width. The wedge is geometric, so a 2 mm solitaire band and a 6 mm wide band are held by the same mechanism. The faces of the slit compress either way — more on the thick band, less on the thin one — but the compression is not what is holding the ring, so grip does not collapse on the thin band.

The critical dimension is therefore not the slit's length alone but the relationship between the slit and the smallest ring in the range. A slit sized from the largest ring lets the smallest one fall straight through. That is exactly what the first sample did.

One slit, four sizes: the numbers

The slit that shipped is 16 mm long, cut 12 mm deep into a 22 mm pad, leaving a 10 mm floor so nothing can drop out of the bottom of the box.

Against that slit:

  • A size J ring (OD 16.8 mm) wedges at a depth of 5.8 mm, standing 11.0 mm above the pad.
  • A size R ring (OD 20.6 mm) wedges at 3.8 mm, standing 16.8 mm above the pad.

The larger ring sits nearly 6 mm taller than the smaller one, on the same slit, with the same hold. Both are gripped at the two ends of the slit; neither is touching the bottom of it. The 10 mm floor exists purely so that the failure mode is never "the ring went through".

The first sample's slit was 20 mm — cut to the largest ring in the range on the reasoning that it had to admit the biggest one. It admitted the biggest one and it also admitted the smallest: at OD 16.8 mm the ring never reached a width of 20 mm, so nothing wedged, and it dropped the full 12 mm to the slit floor. It was held. It was also buried, standing less than 5 mm proud of the pad, and a customer would have had to dig it out with a fingernail. Photographs of that sample look fine, which is the reason it is worth measuring rather than looking.

One shell, two pads

Rings and pendants need different interiors, so the box was built as one shell and two interchangeable pads. Both pads are the same footprint and the same 90 mm square, so they drop into the same base, use the same print and the same carton, and differ only in what is cut into the top face.

  • Ring pad: 22 mm EVA, wrapped in suedette, with the 16 mm slit described above.
  • Pendant pad: 24 mm EVA, with an 8 mm deep recess for the pendant and a serpentine channel for the chain.

The channel is the second place where choosing a resilient material paid off. It is cut 5 mm wide and 7 mm deep, with its mouth narrower than its body. Board cannot do that — a slot in board is the same width all the way down — but EVA deflects, so the chain can be pressed in at assembly speed and will not come back out when the box is inverted or dropped. The first attempt was 8 mm wide and 6 mm deep: the chain went in easily and came out in transit, tangled around the pendant, on two of the three drop units.

Both pads are cut 0.5 mm oversize per side and dropped in rather than glued. Oversize in EVA means they seat under light compression and can still be lifted out with a fingernail, which matters at 500 per SKU: a mis-picked pad is corrected in four seconds instead of scrapping a finished box.

The lid depth, set by the tallest

One box for a range is sized by the tallest configuration in the range, and it is worth being explicit about which that is, because it is not the item that looks tallest.

The pad top sits 12 mm below the base's rim. A size R ring with an 8 mm setting stands 24.8 mm above the pad, so its top is 12.8 mm above the rim. The tallest pendant in its recess stands 22 mm above a pad whose top is 10 mm below the rim, so 12 mm above it. The ring wins by 0.8 mm.

The lid's interior depth is 18 mm, which leaves just over 5 mm above the tallest configuration. Five rather than two because a drop is not a static measurement: the lid panel deflects downward on impact, and at 2 mm the setting marks the interior. The alternative — a deeper lid — was rejected because exterior height is freight, paid once per carton and again per pallet, and because a deeper lid on a pendant SKU is 20 mm of visibly empty air.

One limitation is worth stating rather than glossing over. A slit holds a ring at two points, so the ring can still pivot about the axis of the slit, and under a drop a ring with a tall setting can swing. That is why the lid interior is lined in a suedette-laminated paper rather than the coated wrap used on the exterior, and why the drop test was run on the largest ring with the tallest setting rather than on a representative one.

Structure and specification

  • Structure: two-piece rigid box, lid-and-base. Interior 90 × 90 mm, base interior depth 34 mm, lid interior depth 18 mm.
  • Board: 1.5 mm greyboard throughout. At this size the panels are short enough that 1.5 mm is genuinely stiff, and the exterior dimension matters more than the load on a 90 mm box.
  • Wrap: 128 g coated paper, printed one colour plus a foil logo on the lid, matte lamination. Matte over soft-touch because these boxes are handled in a shop and soft-touch marks permanently.
  • Interior: the lid lined in a suedette-laminated paper, for the pivot described above.
  • Inserts: two EVA pads, wrapped and drop-in, cut with one steel-rule die each. No mould tooling.
  • Quantity: around 3,500 boxes across seven SKUs, in one box size.

What went wrong, and what it cost

The slit was sized from the largest ring. Described above. The cost was one round of sampling and, more annoyingly, a pad that was otherwise correct: the fix was a new die, not a new design, but a steel-rule die is still a tool and it still has to be made. The rule that came out of it — size the slit against the smallest item, check it against the largest — is now how we quote any slot insert.

The wrap was changed after the dieline was cut. The textured stock the brand preferred is thicker than the 128 g coated the dieline was drawn for, and the extra thickness on the base's exterior closed the lid clearance by roughly 0.3 mm. The lid still closed; it fought, and on a two-piece box a lid that fights is the complaint you get after delivery rather than a defect you catch in the factory. The lid clearance had to be opened in the dieline and the sample re-run. That is a dieline decision, which is why the wrap is worth settling before the structure is drawn rather than after it.

What to take from it

  • Size a slot insert against the smallest item in the range, not the largest. A slit longer than the smallest item's width wedges nothing, and the item drops to the floor of the slot.
  • A wedge holds a range; a cavity holds one size. If your SKUs differ in one dimension and are otherwise similar, a slit that no item can pass is cheaper than a cavity per SKU and removes a class of mis-pack.
  • Resilient inserts can be undercut, board cannot. A channel with a mouth narrower than its body takes a chain at assembly speed and keeps it in transit. That is a material property, not a tolerance.
  • Work out which configuration is actually tallest before setting the lid depth. It is often not the obvious one — here a ring beat a pendant by 0.8 mm.
  • Leave about 5 mm above the tallest item, not 2 mm. A lid panel deflects on impact, and the thing it deflects onto is the fragile part of the product.
  • Settle the wrap before the dieline. A thicker stock closes the lid clearance, and a lid that fights is a defect that only shows up in a customer's hands.
  • Make pads drop-in rather than glued when several SKUs share one shell. It costs nothing and it makes a mis-pack recoverable.

The finished range ships in one box, and the smallest ring in it is held as firmly as the largest. The transferable part is not the jewellery — it is the reasoning: when a range varies in one dimension, do not fit the smallest item into a space sized for the largest. Find the dimension that no item in the range can pass, and let the geometry do the holding.
