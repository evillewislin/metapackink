Most rigid box briefs that name a board thickness name the same one: 2 mm. Not because 2 mm was calculated for that box, but because the last box the brand made used 2 mm, or because a supplier once said it was standard. It usually works well enough. When it does not, the failure is not dramatic — the box feels lighter than it looks, the lid rocks slightly, corners start to open in a warehouse — and by then the dieline exists and changing the board means reworking everything downstream of it.

Thickness is a structural variable with four real inputs: what the box carries, how big its panels are, how many boxes will sit on top of it, and how the customer opens it. Working through those four in order takes about ten minutes and produces a number you can defend. This is the order we use when a brief arrives without one, and the reasoning behind each.

Why 2 mm became the default

The commercial range for rigid box greyboard runs roughly from 1.5 mm to 3 mm, and 2 mm sits in the middle of it. That is not an accident. It is the right answer for the most common case: a box around 150 to 250 mm on its longest side, carrying under half a kilo, opened on a table, shipped a dozen to a carton. Inside that envelope almost nothing goes wrong, so habit is a perfectly reasonable heuristic.

The trouble is that the envelope is narrower than people assume, and nothing tells you when you have left it. A box can be inside it on product weight and well outside it on panel size, which is why "it worked last time" is unreliable in a way that is hard to notice: the box does not break, it just feels cheaper than it cost.

What board thickness actually does

Four things, only one of which is strength in the everyday sense.

  • Panel stiffness. A board panel's resistance to bending rises with roughly the cube of its thickness. Going from 1.5 mm to 2.0 mm makes a panel about two and a half times stiffer; going to 3.0 mm makes it roughly eight times stiffer than 1.5 mm. This is the single most powerful lever available, and the most expensive one.
  • Crush resistance. Thicker board resists the concentrated loads that arrive at corners and along edges — the loads that come from being stacked, clamped and dropped rather than from the product sitting inside.
  • Dimensions. The interior is fixed by your product; the board is added outside it. Every 0.5 mm of board adds 1 mm to the exterior length and width, and on a two-piece box the lid has to grow with the base. On a 200 mm box, moving from 2.0 mm to 3.0 mm board adds roughly 4 mm to two dimensions and on the order of five per cent to the exterior volume — which is what freight is charged on once the boxes are in export cartons.
  • Weight. Greyboard is by far the heaviest component of a rigid box. Board weight scales with thickness, so a large box in 3 mm board can weigh close to double the same box in 1.5 mm, and that weight is paid for twice: once in material and once in freight.

One thing thickness does not buy is perceived quality on its own. A heavy box that flexes reads worse than a lighter box that does not, and a reader lifting the lid notices flexing long before they notice weight.

Input one: what the box carries

Four bands cover most projects, and they are wider than most people expect:

  • Under 150 g — jewellery, a small cosmetic, an accessory, a folded garment. Board can go to the bottom of the range without consequence; the product is not the load.
  • 150 to 500 g — a single skincare item, a candle, one perfume bottle, a small set. The default band, and the one 2 mm is genuinely right for.
  • 500 g to 1.5 kg — multiple glass bottles, a spirits bottle, a large vessel candle. Here the static weight is less important than what happens to that weight in transit.
  • Over 1.5 kg — the point at which the structure usually needs rethinking rather than thickening.

Worth being precise about glass. A 430 g bottle does not need thick board because it weighs 430 g; it needs it because in a drop that mass arrives as an impulse several times its own weight, and glass does not absorb any of it. The board and the insert share that job, and which of the two does more of it is a design decision rather than a fixed rule.

Input two: how big the panels are

Stiffness falls faster than area grows, so a panel twice as long is far more than twice as flexible. In practice the longest unsupported span is what matters, and the lid is usually the worst case because it is wide, shallow and has nothing behind it.

Two working rules:

  • Above roughly 250 mm on the longest panel, treat 2 mm as the floor regardless of how little the product weighs. A large box in thin board feels flimsy empty, and nothing you put inside it will fix that.
  • Above roughly 350 mm, or on any wide shallow lid, either go to 2.5 mm or add a stiffener. A bowed lid is not only a feel problem: on a magnetic box it opens a gap at the centre of the closure and the magnets stop holding evenly.

Input three: how many boxes sit on top of it

The bottom box in a carton of twenty carries nineteen boxes. The bottom carton on a pallet carries every carton above it. Export adds a container stack on top of that, and a long sea transit adds humidity, which softens board — greyboard that arrives at a higher moisture content is measurably less stiff than the same board measured dry.

So the question that moves the number is not how much the product weighs but how the finished box travels:

  • Parcel, one box at a time — no stack at all. A direct-to-customer box can often be specified a step thinner than the retail version of the same box.
  • Retail cartons, palletised — the stack dominates the specification, and it dominates it more than the product does.
  • Sea freight with a long transit — add for humidity, and ask what the board's moisture content is at packing rather than assuming it.

Input four: how the customer opens it

This is the input most often left out of a brief, and it is the one that explains the complaint "the box felt cheap even though it was heavy".

  • Lift-off lid. Almost no load on the structure. The board only has to hold its own shape.
  • Magnetic closure. The lid is peeled open against the magnets, repeatedly, over the life of the box. That peeling load goes into the lid's front panel and the skirt, and thin board flexes there in a way that eventually loosens the closure.
  • Drawer box. The pull puts a bending load along the drawer front. A thin drawer front bows as it is pulled, and a bowed front is why trays bind.
  • Carried by the base. The base panel spans between the walls with the whole loaded weight on it. Any flex here is felt immediately, in the hand, by the customer.

What going too thin looks like

Under-specifying rarely produces a broken box. It produces a box that is subtly wrong in several places at once:

  • The lid rocks or creaks when the box is moved, because the lid panel is bowing rather than sitting flat.
  • Corners crush in the carton — usually the bottom boxes, usually starting as a softness rather than a visible dent.
  • The wrap wrinkles toward the centre of a large panel, because the board behind it is flexing.
  • The box sounds hollow when tapped, which a customer reads as cheap before they have opened it.
  • The base bows when the box is picked up one-handed, which is how most customers pick it up.
  • The lid gap opens at the corners after repeated opening, and on a magnetic box the closure loses its evenness.

All of these appear after packing rather than before it, which is what makes thin board expensive to discover.

What going too thick costs you

The opposite error is quieter and more common than people expect, because it looks like caution:

  • Freight. The exterior grows, and exterior volume is the number the freight invoice is built from. It is the largest single cost of over-specifying and the least visible on a sample.
  • Corner bulk. The wrap has further to turn, so corners come out rounder and heavier. On a small box that reads as clumsy rather than solid.
  • Closing fit. A deep-skirted lid over thick board needs more clearance. Overshoot and the lid rocks, which is the same symptom as being too thin.
  • Size bands. Thick board can push an exterior dimension past a postal band, a shelf depth or a carton count, and that step change costs far more than the board does.
  • Material cost. Board is bought by weight and area. Thickness is not a rounding error on a run of several thousand.

Mixing thickness, and the alternative to it

The base and the lid do not have to be the same board. The base carries the product and the stack; the lid mostly has to hold its shape and close flat. Specifying the base at 2.5 mm and the lid at 2.0 mm puts the material where it works and takes weight off the part the customer lifts — and nobody has ever detected it by looking at the box.

There is also an alternative to thickening at all. A wrapped board stiffener under a wide lid panel, or a double-wrapped base panel, will usually out-perform a blanket upgrade at lower material cost. The trade-off is an extra assembly step, so it is a better answer at higher quantities than at low ones.

One more lever worth knowing: board density varies by grade at the same thickness. A dense 1.8 mm board can be stiffer than a light 2.0 mm one. If a supplier quotes thickness in millimetres and nothing else, ask what grade it is — thickness without grade is not a specification you can hold them to.

Thickness changes the wrap, so decide it before the dieline

Three dieline dimensions are set by the board: the corner allowance, the lid clearance and the wrap turn over the top edge. Change the board after the dieline is drawn and all three move, and the corner is where the change shows first — a wrap allowance calculated for 2 mm board will pull back on 3 mm.

There is a pairing effect too. Very heavy board under a light, smooth wrap tends to show the board's surface through it. If you go up in board, expect to go up in wrap weight as well.

How to specify it so the quotation means something

  • State thickness in millimetres with a tolerance. A tenth of a millimetre either way is normal in production; without a stated tolerance there is nothing to measure against.
  • Say whether the lid and the base are the same. If they are not, say which is which.
  • Name the grade, or ask the supplier to name it and hold it. Thickness alone is not a complete specification.
  • Say how the box ships and how it opens. Both change the answer, and neither is guessable from the dimensions.
  • Ask for the resulting exterior dimensions. They are what you will pay to ship, and they are the number that decides carton counts.
  • Check it on the sample with a calliper, at a corner and at the middle of a panel, because board is sometimes thinner at the fold than at the face.

None of this turns a buyer into a packaging engineer. It turns a number copied from the last project into a number with a reason behind it — which is what makes the difference between a box that feels right and a box that costs slightly more than it needed to feel slightly worse.
