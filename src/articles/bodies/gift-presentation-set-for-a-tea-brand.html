The brief asked for a rigid box. We quoted the rigid box, and we also quoted a folding carton, and we said which one we thought they should buy. They bought the rigid box from somebody else. This is written up because the reasoning is reusable, because it applies to a whole class of products that keep getting rigid boxes they do not need, and because the brand came back nine months later and bought the carton anyway.

Names, artwork and commercial figures are left out. Board weights, dimensions, insert geometry, quantity bands and the volume comparison are not, because those are the parts a buyer with a similar product can reuse.

What was being boxed

  • Three 100 g caddies of loose-leaf tea, in a gift presentation set. Each caddy is a drawn aluminium cylinder, 73 mm diameter, 62 mm tall, with a slip lid, weighing about 185 g filled.
  • Total contents: 555 g, sitting on a flat base, in three rigid metal cylinders.
  • Two runs: a launch run of around 12,000, and a seasonal gifting range expected a few months later.

The brief specified a two-piece rigid box: 1.5 mm greyboard, 128 g coated wrap, matte black with a foil logo, and an EVA insert with three circular cavities. It is a perfectly reasonable specification. It is also a specification that answers a problem this product does not have, and it is worth being precise about which problem that is.

What a rigid box is actually for

A rigid box costs several times what a carton costs, and it buys four things: load-carrying, crush resistance, a structure that survives being kept, and an edge quality you cannot get from a fold. The question is never whether a rigid box is better. It is whether any of those four is load-bearing in this particular project.

Taken against this product:

  • Load-carrying: not needed. 555 g spread across three cylinders standing on a flat panel. There is no concentrated load, no fragile material and nothing that can be crushed by the box's own structure.
  • Crush resistance: partly needed, and a carton has it. Board is strong in compression along a vertical wall. A carton stacked on a pallet carries load through its walls at least as well as a rigid box does, because both fail at the corners and neither fails in the middle of a panel.
  • Being kept: not applicable. The caddies are what gets kept. The box is a shelf-presentation object and a transit object, and it is recycled the week the tea is opened. That is the sentence that decided the recommendation.
  • Edge quality: real, and it is the one the carton loses. A rigid box has wrapped, mitred corners with no visible fold. A carton has a crease at every corner. On matte black, that difference is visible, and it is honest to say so up front.

There was also a constraint in the brief that the rigid specification quietly contradicted. The brand's own site described its packaging as plastic-free. An EVA insert is not plastic-free, it has to be taken out before the box is recycled, and a customer who reads that claim and then finds a foam block inside has been told something untrue by the packaging.

The alternative: a telescoping carton

The carton we quoted keeps the one gesture the brand wanted. A rigid two-piece box opens by lifting the lid away in one movement, and that is the moment the brief was written around. So the carton is not a tuck box — it is a telescoping carton: a tray and a separate slipcase lid, the lid lifting off the top of the tray exactly as a rigid lid does.

  • Board: 450 gsm solid bleached board, rather than the 350 gsm a tuck carton of this size would normally use. The extra weight is what gives it the stiffness a lift-off lid needs.
  • Finish: matte lamination outside, aqueous coating inside, foil-blocked logo, shallow blind emboss.
  • Base: crash-lock (automatic) bottom. At 555 g a standard tuck bottom is not a load-carrying base; the four interlocked flaps of a crash-lock are.
  • Exterior: 231 × 87 × 71 mm, against 236 × 92 × 76 mm for the rigid box it replaced. Five millimetres narrower, five shallower, because the walls are 0.6 mm of board instead of 1.5 mm of greyboard plus a wrap.

The numbers

Three figures, all at the 12,000-unit quantity, and all of them moving the same way.

  • Unit cost. The carton came in somewhere in the region of 35 to 45 per cent of the rigid box at the same size and finish. That is mostly the wrap: a rigid box is greyboard, adhesive and a sheet of printed paper applied panel by panel by hand, and the hand operation is the expensive part.
  • Tooling. Both need a steel-rule cutting die, and there the similarity ends. The carton needed one die for the tray and lid and one for the platform. The rigid box needed board dies, and the wrapping setup that goes with a hand-wrapped box. The carton's tooling was roughly a third of the rigid's.
  • Freight, which is the one nobody budgets for. Rigid boxes ship assembled. At 236 × 92 × 76 mm, packed 24 to a shipper, 12,000 units is about 16 m³. The same quantity of cartons, shipped flat as blanks, is about 1.2 m³ — a ratio in the region of thirteen to one. On a sea leg that is a rounding error. On an air freight leg to meet a launch date, it is the difference between a freight line and a freight problem, and it is also a warehousing line: the brand's 3PL was going to store 16 m³ of empty boxes.

What the carton gives back is stated above and repeated here because it should be decided deliberately rather than discovered: roughly 230 g of heft, and a crease at each corner instead of a wrapped edge. On a gift set the customer picks up and judges by weight, that is a real cost.

Holding three tins without foam

Three cylinders side by side in a rectangular box look like they hold each other. The two outer ones are held by the walls and the middle one by its neighbours, so the interior only needs to be three diameters long. That is the obvious solution and it has a tolerance problem worth spelling out.

A tolerance across three items accumulates. If the interior is drawn as three diameters plus a working allowance, and each tin is 0.4 mm under nominal, the far end has 1.2 mm of free play — and it is not distributed evenly. The outer two are pressed against the walls; the middle one is floating between two things that are themselves loose. On a metal caddy in a board box, 1.2 mm of free play is an audible rattle when the box is picked up.

The insert that shipped is a window platform: a die-cut sheet with three 74.2 mm circular holes, sitting 18 mm above the base floor on four folded flaps. Each caddy passes through its own hole and stands on the base floor beneath it. Because each hole positions its own caddy independently of the other two, nothing accumulates — the tolerance on the middle caddy is the same 1.2 mm as the outer ones, not three times it.

Two details in that platform are worth keeping:

  • The hole is deeper than its clearance. 1.2 mm of lateral play in a hole 18 mm deep limits a caddy to about 4° of tilt, which is why 1.2 mm is acceptable here and would not be in a shallow 6 mm recess. The play is not the specification; the ratio of play to depth is.
  • It needs no adhesive and no tooling beyond the die. The four flaps fold down and stand on the base floor, and the caddies' own weight keeps the platform seated. Nothing is glued, so nothing has to cure, and the whole insert is one part from one die.

Making a carton read as a rigid box

Three things do most of the work, and one of them is a compromise the brand had to accept.

Weight in the board. 450 gsm rather than 350, which is the difference between a box that feels like a carton and one that does not collapse when it is picked up by its lid.

A lift-off lid. The telescoping construction, which is most of the perceived quality. A tuck-top carton at any board weight reads as a carton.

The finish carried across unchanged. The same matte lamination and the same foil blocking the rigid box would have had, on the same artwork.

The compromise is the emboss. The brand's logo was drawn expecting a deep deboss, and 450 gsm board cannot hold the depth a 1.5 mm greyboard takes — it holds a shallow blind emboss and no more. We reduced it and paired it with the foil, and it is worth saying plainly that this is a place where the carton gives something up rather than a place where it is equivalent.

What went wrong, and what it cost

The first platform holes were cut at 73.5 mm. Half a millimetre over the nominal caddy diameter, which sounds right and jammed on the line. A drawn aluminium caddy is not a nominal cylinder: the rolled seam at the top stands proud and the diameter varies by about ±0.3 mm, so 73.5 mm was an interference fit on part of the run. It was opened to 74.2 mm. The cost was one new die — cheap in itself, and the reason that number is measured across ten caddies now rather than taken off a drawing.

The emboss pushed through into the lid's sliding face. An emboss raised on the outside of the slipcase raises a matching ridge on the inside, and the inside of the slipcase is the face that slides over the tray. The lid caught on the ridge just before it seated. Lowering the emboss solved it, and the rule that came out of it is worth keeping: on a telescoping carton, no raised finish on the lid's outer face below the point where the tray's top edge travels.

The slipcase clearance was drawn at 0.3 mm. A box-within-a-box needs the same clearance a rigid lid does, and 0.3 mm per side fought once the board had taken up a little moisture in the warehouse. It was opened to 0.6 mm per side, which is the same figure a rigid lid-and-base box uses, for the same reason.

Structure and specification

  • Structure: telescoping folding carton — crash-lock base tray plus a separate slipcase lid. Exterior 231 × 87 × 71 mm.
  • Board: 450 gsm solid bleached board throughout. Matte lamination outside, aqueous coating inside.
  • Finishing: foil-blocked logo, shallow blind emboss, no raised finish on the lid below the tray's travel line.
  • Insert: one die-cut window platform, 450 gsm board, three 74.2 mm holes, 18 mm stand-off on four folded flaps. No adhesive, no mould tooling.
  • Clearances: 0.6 mm per side between tray and slipcase; 1.2 mm on the caddy holes.
  • Quantity: 6,500 units in the run that shipped. The 12,000-unit launch run went to a supplier who quoted the rigid box as asked.
  • Recyclability: board and board, one stream, no insert to remove.

What to take from it

  • Ask what the rigid box is for before agreeing to one. Load, crush, being kept, and edge quality. If none of the four is load-bearing in the project, the cost difference is real money spent on nothing.
  • Read the brand's own sustainability claim against the insert. A plastic-free claim and an EVA insert contradict each other, and the customer is the one who finds out.
  • Budget freight on assembled rigid boxes. Flat cartons against assembled boxes is a ratio in the region of thirteen to one, and it is charged twice — once on the way in, once as warehousing.
  • Do not let three items hold each other. Tolerance accumulates across a row, and the middle item is the loose one. Give each item its own hole.
  • Specify hole clearance against hole depth, not as an absolute. 1.2 mm of play is fine in an 18 mm deep hole and a rattle in a 6 mm one.
  • Measure the real product, not the drawing. A drawn caddy varies by ±0.3 mm and its seam stands proud; half a millimetre over nominal is an interference fit.
  • Keep raised finishes off a sliding face. An emboss on the outside of a slipcase is a ridge on the inside, and the inside is what slides.
  • Say so, and quote both. We lost the larger order by recommending the carton and got it back nine months later on a six-week window the rigid box could not have met. The recommendation was correct either way.

The set ships in a carton, in one material, recycled in one piece, at roughly a third of the freight volume. The transferable part is not tea — it is the question. When a brief asks for a rigid box, the useful answer is not yes or no but a list of what a rigid box actually buys, checked against what the product actually needs. Tea in metal caddies needs none of it, and a great many products are tea in metal caddies.
