The sample stage is where most packaging projects are won or lost, and it is the stage buyers understand least. It arrives as a line on a quotation — a fee, a number of days, a box in the post — and it is usually read as a formality between agreeing a price and placing an order. It is not a formality. It is the only point in the project at which a change costs a drawing revision rather than a container of finished goods.

This is what actually happens during it, what the fee pays for, what the clock runs on, what to do the day the box arrives, and how to tell whether a second round is worth paying for. Two of those answers are counter-intuitive and both of them are about cost.

A sample is a reference, not a prototype

The word prototype carries the wrong implication. A prototype implies something you iterate towards a final design, and the usual expectation is that it will be rough. A packaging sample is the opposite: it is built on the same tooling, from the same materials, by the same people who will make the production run, and it is meant to be identical to what you will receive.

That is the whole reason it has value. When a production shipment is disputed eight weeks later, the question is not "is this box good enough"; it is "is this box the box that was approved". An approved sample is the answer to that question in physical form, and a sample approved on a photograph is not an answer at all.

So the sample has two jobs at once. It is a decision tool for you — the only way to know whether a lid fits or a bottle stays put — and it is a contract document for both sides. Everything in this article follows from taking the second job as seriously as the first.

What the sample fee actually pays for

A sample is not a box. It is a box produced at a quantity of one, and almost every cost in making a box is a cost that does not shrink with quantity. Four things are being paid for, and none of them is profit.

  • Machine setup. A press does not care whether it prints one sheet or five hundred. The plates are mounted, the ink is brought up to density, the register is set and the waste is thrown away before the first usable sheet comes off. One sheet costs most of what five hundred cost.
  • Tooling. A cutting die has to be made before anything can be cut, and a dieline that has never been made before has to be proved — cut, checked against the drawing, and adjusted. On a structure we have made before, that step is mostly a lookup rather than a development.
  • Materials at minimum. Wrap paper is bought by the ream and greyboard by the sheet. A stock that is not on the shelf has a mill minimum that is usually far larger than one box needs, and that minimum is a real cost whether or not you proceed.
  • Engineering time. Someone has to convert a description into a dieline, choose the corner allowance for the actual wrap, and set the insert cavity against the actual product. That is the part a cheap quotation skips, and it is the part that shows up later as a lid that does not fit.

Why it is charged rather than given away

The obvious question is why a supplier who expects a production order would not absorb a few hundred in sampling to win it. Some do, and it is worth knowing what a free sample tells you.

A sample given away is a sample built to be cheap. In practice that means a stock structure rather than yours, a wrap from the shelf rather than the one you specified, and a hand-finished unit made by the most skilled person on the floor rather than by the line that will make your run. It will look excellent. It will tell you very little about the box you are going to buy.

There is a second reason, which is about who is committing to what. A fee is a small commitment on both sides: the buyer is serious enough to pay for a decision, and the supplier is obligated to produce the thing that was described rather than an approximation of it. A project where nobody has paid anything is a project where nobody is yet committed, and the sampling stage is where that matters most, because it is where the specification is settled.

Credited against the order is not the same as refundable

Most suppliers, including us, credit the sample fee against the first production order: the amount appears as a deduction on the bulk invoice, so in the normal case — sample approved, order placed — the sampling ends up costing nothing.

That is why it gets described as a deposit. It is not one, and the distinction costs people money. A deposit is held and returned; a sample fee is spent. The die exists, the wrap minimum has been bought and the press has been set up, and if the project stops there is nothing to hand back. So the honest way to read the credit is as a discount on the order you place, not as a refund you are entitled to.

One question worth asking before you pay: whether the credit survives a change of structure. If the first sample is a two-piece box and the second is a drawer box, the tooling is different and the fee usually is not credited twice.

The white sample: structure before print

A white sample — sometimes called a dummy or a plain sample — is the structure made up in an unprinted stock. No print, no foil, no embossing. It exists to answer the questions that print is irrelevant to and that artwork would otherwise slow down.

Three things are settled on it, and all three are cheaper to settle here than anywhere else:

  • Whether the product fits. With the real product in hand and the real cavity in front of you, the question of clearance stops being a number on a drawing. A bottle either releases from the cradle one-handed or it does not.
  • Whether the exterior is the size you want. The exterior is what you pay to ship, and it is the dimension that surprises people. Board thickness, wrap and closure all add to it, and a box drawn to an interior is always bigger outside than expected.
  • Whether the opening works. A lid that lifts, a drawer that slides, a magnet that holds — all mechanical, all unaffected by what is printed on them.

On a new structure we would rather make a white sample first and a printed one second. It costs one extra round and it usually saves one, because the failure it finds is always a dimensional one, and a dimensional change after the artwork is laid out against the dieline means re-laying the artwork.

The printed sample, and what the proof is not

The printed sample is the full specification: your wrap, your print, your finishing, your insert. This is the unit that gets approved, and the unit the production run is measured against.

The trap in it is colour. A digital proof on a calibrated screen is not the colour you will get, and neither is a press proof on a different stock. The wrap is stretched around corners, laminated, and often printed on a paper whose white point is not the white point of the proof. If brand colour is contractual, approve it on the sample under the light the product will be sold under — a shop floor, not a desk — and say so, because the lights in a factory and the lights in a showroom are two different colours.

Two other things to check on the printed sample that no proof shows: whether the artwork bleeds past the fold enough that no bare board appears at the corners, and whether two finishes specified on the same panel have compromised each other. Foil on a textured stock, spot UV on a matte lamination, deboss into a heavy grain — each of those either works on this material or does not, and the only way to find out is to press it.

Where tooling enters, and what you own

A steel-rule cutting die is made for your dieline and kept for your reorders. It is a one-off charge, it is why the first order costs more than the second, and it is usually kept for a defined period — ask how long, and ask what happens to it after.

What is worth being precise about is which changes reuse the tooling and which do not:

  • Artwork only: reuses everything. Same die, same wrap setup, new plates. This is the cheap change and the one to aim for.
  • A finish added or removed: reuses the die, may need a new block or screen.
  • A dimension changed: new die. The corner allowance, the lid clearance and the wrap turn-in are all cut from the same tool.
  • An insert changed: new die for the insert only, if it is die-cut board. A moulded pulp insert is a different and much larger tooling conversation.

The practical consequence: settle dimensions before artwork. Artwork is the cheap thing to change and the structure is the expensive one, which is the opposite of the order most projects run in.

What the clock actually runs on

Every sampling time you are quoted is measured from approved artwork, not from the enquiry. That single sentence explains most of the schedule slippage buyers experience, because the weeks between the first email and the approved file are not part of anybody's lead time — they are part of the project, but they are not part of the clock.

Three things move the clock once it has started, and all three are under your control:

  • Whether the structure is new. A dieline we have cut before starts as a lookup. A structure being developed for the first time has to be drawn, cut, measured and corrected before the sample is even made.
  • Whether the material is on the shelf. A standard coated paper is there. A specific textured stock, a fabric, or a mill-branded paper may have to be bought in, and that wait is usually longer than the making.
  • How many finishes are involved. Each finish that needs its own block, screen or plate adds a setup, and two finishes on the same panel sometimes need a sequence rather than a single pass.

What does not move it is how quickly you approve. A sample sitting on a desk for a fortnight is a fortnight, and the two things most likely to cost you a launch date are an unapproved artwork and an unreturned sample.

What to check the day the sample arrives

Do not open it on a desk. Open it the way the customer will, and then do the things the customer will not.

  • Load it with the real product. Not a stand-in, not an empty bottle, not the same bottle in a different size. The real, filled, capped item, at the heaviest specification in the range.
  • Measure it. Board with a calliper at a corner and at the middle of a panel; the exterior with a ruler; the interior against what you asked for. Ten minutes, and it is the only way to know whether the thing you were quoted is the thing you received.
  • Close it and carry it by the base. Then turn it upside down. These two movements catch most closure and cradle problems, and neither is visible in a photograph.
  • Look at the corners and the interior. Corner separation starts as a corner that was never quite tight. The interior is where the wrap turns in, and it is where the assembly short cuts show.
  • Smell it. Close the empty box, leave it somewhere warm, open it. A box with a smell will give that smell to the product, and this is the check nobody does until it is too late.

What a photograph cannot tell you

Suppliers send photographs of samples, and buyers approve from them, because the alternative is a courier wait. It is worth being explicit about what those photographs cannot show.

Fit, first: a lid with the right clearance and a lid with too much look identical, and the difference is between a box that opens with one hand and a box whose lid falls off in a parcel. Force, second: a magnet that holds under a base carry and one that does not are the same magnet in the same position, and the photograph shows the position. Odour, third, which no image carries at all. And stiffness, fourth: whether a 250 mm panel in 1.5 mm board flexes when you pick it up loaded is the single most common complaint about a box that looked right in every picture.

There is a fair compromise when the wait matters. Ask for a video of the specific checks — the lid being lifted one-handed, the box being inverted, the product being lifted out of the cavity — rather than a set of studio stills. It is not the same as holding it, but it answers the four questions above, which the stills do not.

When a second round is worth paying for

A second sample is worth it when the change is a dimension, when the product has changed, or when the first sample failed a test that matters.

Dimensional changes are the good case. A cradle that grips 2 mm too high, a cavity that holds the jar but lets it rotate, a slit cut to the wrong ring size — these are single numbers on a drawing, and a revised sample proves the new number. The second round is also the right answer when the product moved: a formulation changed the bottle, a supplier changed the cap, and everything downstream has to be re-proved against the new object.

And it is obviously right when the first sample failed something physical: the lid lifted, the magnet let go, the foil flaked under tape, the drawer bound. Those are defects, not preferences, and no amount of discussion resolves them. A revised sample does.

When it is not

Three situations where a second sample costs money and decides nothing.

The change is in the artwork. A print proof on the specified stock settles it, and a proof is cheaper and faster than a box. Sample the structure once and iterate the artwork separately.

The brief is unresolved. "Make it feel more premium" is not a specification, and a second sample will not resolve it — it will produce a second box that also feels wrong, for reasons nobody can name. The move here is not another round; it is to name the thing that is actually being asked for. Heavier board, a softer wrap, deeper emboss, a slower open — each is a different specification and each is a decision, not a feeling.

It is the third round. By the third sample the problem is almost never the box. It is that the structure, the product and the price were never all agreed at once, and each round is discovering that again. The honest move at that point is to stop sampling and go back to the brief — it is cheaper than the fourth box and it is the only thing that has any chance of ending the loop.

What signing the sample commits you to

An approval is a specification. Anything not recorded on it is not part of the specification, however many emails preceded it — and this is the sentence that decides packaging disputes, because a verbal agreement about board thickness is unenforceable against a production run and a written one is not.

The approval should record: the board thickness at each part, the wrap stock and its finish, the exterior dimensions, the insert material and its cavity dimensions, the closure specification including magnet count and position, the colour reference it was approved against, and the date. Sign it, date it, keep a copy, and make sure the copy you keep is the one that was signed rather than a later one that was not.

Then there is the question people ask less often: what happens if production does not match it. The answer should be agreed before the order, not after the shipment, and it is worth asking what the supplier's own inspection measures against. A supplier who can tell you what was measured is a supplier whose process you can reason about; one who can only tell you it passed is not.

The sampling checklist

  • Ask what the fee covers — tooling, materials, setup, and whether it is credited against the order or refundable. These are different promises.
  • Sample the structure before the artwork. A white sample finds dimensional problems while they are still free to fix.
  • Settle dimensions before the dieline is drawn. Artwork is cheap to change and structure is not, which is the reverse of the usual order.
  • Approve colour on the sample, under the light it will be sold under. A screen is not a reference and a proof on a different stock is not either.
  • Load it, carry it by the base, invert it, measure it and smell it. Five checks that take fifteen minutes and cover most of what a photograph cannot.
  • Sample again for a dimension, not for a feeling. A number gets proved by the next box; a preference does not.
  • Write the specification down and sign it. The approval is the only document that exists in eight weeks' time.

The sampling stage is the cheapest place in a packaging project to be wrong and the most expensive place to be vague. A buyer who knows what the fee buys, what the clock runs on and what the approval commits them to gets through it in one round most of the time — not by being lucky, but by arriving with the product, a ruler and a decision already made.
