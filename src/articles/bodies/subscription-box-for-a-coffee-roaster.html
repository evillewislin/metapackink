The brief was a box for a monthly coffee subscription: a kilo of beans, two bags, a tasting card, shipped direct to the subscriber every month. The roaster had a specification in mind before we spoke — a magnetic closure rigid box — and most of this project was about why the thing that shipped was a two-piece box with a paper band.

Names, artwork and commercial figures are left out. Dimensions, board, wrap, band construction and quantity bands are not, because those are the parts another subscription business can reuse.

What was being shipped, every month

  • Two 500 g gusseted bags, valve-fitted, filled within twenty-four hours of roasting
  • A tasting card, 148 × 105 mm, one per month with different copy
  • Total content weight roughly 1.05 kg; packed weight with the box about 1.4 kg
  • Shipped as the outer parcel — no shipper, no outer carton, no void fill

Two properties of that list decide everything else. The bags are soft and their volume is not fixed. And the box is the parcel, which means the closure has to hold through a courier network rather than through a shelf.

A friction lid is not a shipping closure

A two-piece rigid box holds its lid by friction: the lid's interior is a fraction of a millimetre smaller than the base's exterior, and that interference is what keeps it on. On a shelf it is perfectly adequate. As a parcel it is not a closure at all.

We dropped the first sample — a two-piece box, loaded, no band — from one metre onto a corner. The lid came off on the second drop and landed separately. That is not a defect in the box; friction is a positioning mechanism and a drop is a rotation, and the lid has nothing resisting rotation about the rim. Whatever the interference, the lid cannot be held by a force applied at its own edge.

The brief's answer to this was a magnetic closure, and it is a genuine answer — a positive closure that resists rotation because two magnets hold the front skirt down. We quoted it alongside, and it came back around thirty per cent above the equivalent two-piece box, which is consistent with what magnets and steel plates cost once the skirt has to be recessed and the two halves have to register.

It was not chosen, for a reason that only appears on the twelfth month.

The bags grow after the box is closed

Coffee is packed hot off the roaster and it degasses for about a fortnight, most of it in the first three days. The one-way valve lets CO2 out and keeps oxygen from coming in, so the bag does not burst. It does still change shape: a settled gusseted bag measured about 62 mm across its flat face, and the same bag forty-eight hours after packing measured 72 mm at its most inflated.

Ten millimetres, on a product that was measured before it was packed. The first sample's base interior was 70 mm deep, which looked like 8 mm of clearance over a 62 mm bag and was in fact 2 mm of interference once the bags had inflated — and the interference pushed the lid up from the inside.

Two changes came out of it. The base interior went to 78 mm, which puts 6 mm over the inflated dimension rather than over the settled one. And the roaster moved packing to forty-eight hours after roast, which lets the worst of the degassing happen in a tray rather than in a closed box. The second change was theirs to make and it cost nothing; the first was ours and it cost exterior height.

The general point is worth keeping: with a compressible product whose volume changes after packing, the dimension that matters is the largest one it will reach, not the one it has when it goes in.

Why there is no insert cavity at all

The instinct with two objects in a box is to cut a cavity for each. Here that is the wrong answer for both objects, for the same reason.

A cavity is a fixed volume and the bags are not. Cut it to the settled bag and an inflated bag will not seat; cut it to the inflated bag and a settled bag rattles in it, which is worse, because a bag that moves inside a box arrives at one end of it. So the interior is a plain volume with clearance and no cavity, and the bags locate against the walls and against each other.

They do get one divider: a single folded paperboard sheet standing across the middle of the box, 55 mm tall, unglued, dropped in at packing. Without it both bags migrate to one end over a journey, and a box with all its mass at one end drops onto one corner with the whole load behind it. With it, each bag has its own well and stays roughly where it was packed.

The divider is deliberately shorter than the bags. It separates; it does not capture. A captured bag has to be lifted out over a 55 mm wall, and on a packing line running a few thousand units a month the seconds are the cost that nobody quotes.

The band: closure, tamper evidence and artwork in one part

What replaced the magnet is a printed paper band — a pre-glued open sleeve, 70 mm wide, slid over the box from one end and covering the top face, the bottom face and both short ends.

It does four jobs, which is the reason it is worth more than its cost:

  • It closes the box. A continuous loop around the girth cannot let the lid lift, because a lid moving up is now resisted by the band's tension across the top face. The closure became positive at roughly a tenth of what the magnets added.
  • It is the tamper evidence. A band has to be torn to open the box, and a torn band cannot be put back. On a subscription, where the customer is trusting that nobody has been into their parcel, that is worth having — and it is the reason a band beats a belly-band that slides off intact.
  • It carries the artwork that changes. Month to month, the only thing that changes on this project is the band: a CMYK print of the month's origin, the roast date and the tasting note. The box underneath is identical in month one and month twelve.
  • It is thrown away. Which means the box arrives unlabelled and unmarked, and the customer is holding a clean rigid box rather than a parcel.

Structure and specification

  • Structure: two-piece lid-and-base rigid box. Base interior 240 × 150 × 78 mm; exterior 248 × 158 × 104 mm. Lid skirt 26 mm.
  • Board: 2.0 mm greyboard in the base, 1.5 mm in the lid. The base is specified against the 1.4 kg packed weight and against being stacked in a courier network; the lid carries nothing but its own panel, and putting 2.0 mm up there would add exterior volume to the one part with no load in it.
  • Wrap: 157 gsm coated paper, matte lamination, printed one spot colour on the exterior and not printed at all on the interior face. The unprinted interior is an odour decision rather than a cost one — see below.
  • Band: 250 gsm coated board, CMYK plus matte lamination, cut 818 mm with a 15 mm factory-glued lap, sliding on over the 158 × 104 end with about 2 mm of ease.
  • Divider: 400 gsm folded paperboard, 55 mm tall, dropped in loose.
  • Adhesive: water-based throughout, fully cured before assembly.
  • Quantity: a few thousand a month, ordered quarterly.

Coffee is an odour sponge

Coffee beans are sold as odour absorbers. That is not a metaphor — a bowl of grounds is a standard remedy for a fridge — and it means a box that smells of anything will give that smell to the product.

Three specifications came out of it. Water-based adhesive, cured, because it off-gasses while curing and the box is closed while it does. No foam anywhere, including under the divider. And an unprinted interior face on the wrap, because a heavy solid of ink on the inside of a sealed box is a print note that dissipates on an open shelf and not at all in a closed one.

The test is the same one we run on chocolate: close the empty finished box, leave it twenty-four hours somewhere warm, open it and smell it. It costs nothing and it is the only check that catches this before production.

What the twelfth month does that the first does not

A subscription box is not one box shipped once. It is the same box shipped to the same person twelve times, and that multiplication turns three small things into the specification.

Assembly, multiplied. Anything awkward about packing the box is paid for every month by the same team. A band with a glue lap to be closed on the line would be a jig, an operator and a drying step on a few thousand units a month; a pre-glued sleeve that slides on from one end is one motion. The divider is dropped in loose rather than glued for the same reason. A one-off gift box can absorb an awkward step. A monthly one cannot.

Tooling, divided. The cutting die for the box and the band is charged on the first order and not on the eleven that follow. The twelfth month's box is cheaper per unit than the first even though nothing about it changed, and a subscription business forecasting its unit economics should model that: the packaging cost per shipment falls after month one and then flattens.

Volume, held. At 248 × 158 × 104 mm, a rigid box is about 4 litres assembled, so ten thousand of them is roughly 41 m³ of warehouse. Ordering quarterly at a few thousand a month holds about 31 m³ of empty boxes. That is the argument against buying the annual quantity to get the better unit price — the discount is real and the rent is real too.

Twelve boxes a year into one bin

This is the reason the magnet lost. A subscriber receives twelve of these boxes a year, and each one is opened, emptied and put in the bin within a week. A box that has to be disassembled before it can be recycled is a box that goes in the wrong bin, twelve times, by a customer who likes the brand.

A two-piece rigid box with a paper wrap and a paper band is one material: greyboard and paper, both accepted in kerbside paper in most markets. Add magnets and steel plates and it is not, and the honest options are a disassembly step nobody will do or a contamination complaint the brand owns. On a single purchase that trade-off is arguable. On a monthly one it is not.

What went wrong, and what it cost

The lid came off the first sample. Found on the second drop, fixed by the band, and it is the reason the band exists rather than being decoration. Cost: one revised sample and no retooling, because the box dimensions did not change.

The bags inflated past the interior depth. Found because the sample was held for forty-eight hours loaded before it was approved — a sample approved the day it arrives would have missed it entirely. Cost: 8 mm of base depth, which is 8 mm of exterior height and therefore freight.

The first band tore in transit. The perforated tear line ran the full width of the band, which is a continuous weak line around the whole girth; two of ten units came back with the band split. The fix was a notch at one edge and a perf that stops 8 mm short of each end, so the band is continuous except where a thumb goes. That is the kind of detail no specification review catches and a ten-unit transit test does.

The courier label bridged the band. A label applied across the band and the lid cannot be removed without destroying one or the other, and the subscriber was then peeling paper off a box they were meant to want. The dieline now reserves a clear rectangle on the lid's top face that the band does not cross.

What to take from it

  • Specify the closure for the journey, not the shelf. Friction holds a lid on a shelf. A parcel needs a positive closure, and a band is the cheapest one that is also recyclable.
  • Size the interior against the largest the product will be, not the size it is when packed. Anything that degasses, settles or warms has two volumes and the box only gets the second one.
  • Do not cut a cavity for a compressible product. Give it a volume and a divider. A fixed cavity is either too small on Monday or too big on Friday.
  • Put the variable artwork on the part you throw away. One structure, twelve bands: the die is charged once and the plates are the only thing that changes.
  • Run a ten-unit transit test, not a drop test. The band tear and the label bridge were both found by sending it, and neither would have been found by approving it on a desk.
  • Count the multiplications before you choose the structure. Assembly seconds, tooling amortisation, warehouse volume and recycling friction are all multiplied by twelve on a subscription, and any one of them can be larger than the unit cost you were optimising.

The finished box is unremarkable to look at, which is the point: a subscription box has to be boring to pack, boring to receive and boring to throw away, twelve times over. The interesting decisions were all in the things that repeat.
