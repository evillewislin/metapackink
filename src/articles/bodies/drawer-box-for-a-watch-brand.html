The brief was a drawer box for a mechanical watch: a wrapped tray sliding out of a wrapped sleeve, pulled by a ribbon, with the watch strapped around a cushioned form. Nothing about it was exotic. It is worth writing up because of one decision that drawer box briefs usually get wrong, and that decided this project: where the clearance for the slide comes from.

A drawer has to move, which means the tray cannot touch the sleeve. That gap is a real dimension and it has to be taken out of something. Taken out of the wrong thing, it shows up as a watch that arrives tilted at the back of its own tray, or as a tray that binds after a month at sea.

The watch, and what the box had to do with it

  • A 41 mm stainless steel case on a leather strap, buckled around a cushioned form
  • The cushion: roughly 70 × 70 × 45 mm, fabric-wrapped over an EVA core
  • Watch and cushion together: around 190 g
  • An interior of about 95 × 95 × 60 mm to hold the cushion with room for the strap coil

A watch is a forgiving product in some ways — it is small, rigid and not fragile — and an unforgiving one in others. It is presented on a form rather than in a cavity, the strap is buckled rather than tucked, and the whole assembly has to sit level and centred when the tray comes out. Any slack in the chain between the sleeve and the watch shows up as a watch that is not where it should be.

Where the sliding clearance comes from

There are two dimensions in a drawer box and only one of them is negotiable. The sleeve's interior is set by the sleeve dieline; the tray's exterior is set by the tray dieline; the gap between them is the difference.

The easy move — and the one the first sample made — is to shrink the tray until it slides. It works, in the sense that the tray slides. But shrinking the tray's exterior shrinks its interior, the interior sets the cavity, the cavity sets the cushion, and the cushion is the thing that holds the watch. The clearance has been taken out of the only part of the assembly that cannot afford to give.

The correct move is the other one: hold the tray at the size the cushion needs and take the clearance out of the sleeve, by making the sleeve's interior dimension larger. The sleeve grows; the tray, the cavity, the cushion and the watch all stay at the size the product requires.

It is not free — the sleeve's exterior grows by the same amount, and exterior volume is what freight is charged on. On a box this size, 1.6 mm across the width and height is a rounding error against the exterior volume, and it is far cheaper than the alternative. On a larger drawer box the same trade has to be looked at properly, and on a multi-component set it is the trade that decides the whole project.

The cushion, and why it must not be the thing that gives

This is the part that turned the project from a packaging job into a fitting problem, and it is worth stating plainly because it applies to every drawer box.

A drawer converts a pull on the ribbon into movement of everything inside it. That conversion only works if the fit between the cushion and the cavity is tighter than the slide clearance between the tray and the sleeve. If it is not, the tray comes out and the watch stays where it is — the cushion slides backward inside the tray by exactly the amount the drawer travelled, and the watch arrives at the back of a box that is now visibly wrong.

So the cushion fit has to be positive, not frictional. The cavity is cut 2 mm under the cushion's dimension on each side and the EVA compresses to seat, which gives an interference fit that does not depend on the fabric's coefficient of friction and does not change as the fabric wears. The cushion cannot be lifted out without a deliberate pull, and it cannot shift when the drawer does.

The watch's own fit to the cushion is a separate and much more forgiving question: the strap is buckled around the form, so it is a positive grip rather than a friction one. That is what makes the cushion the right place to spend tolerance and the watch the wrong place to look for it.

The ribbon: anchored under the wrap, not glued to it

The first sample had the ribbon glued to the outer face of the tray's front panel. It held at rest and pulled away at roughly 3 kg — comfortably less than what a customer yanks at when a drawer sticks, and well inside what a courier can apply to a ribbon hanging outside a carton.

The fix is not a stronger adhesive. It is to move the load out of the adhesive altogether: a slot is cut through the tray board, the ribbon end is passed through it and trapped between the board and the wrap, so the pull goes into the board itself. The failure mode moves from the glue line to the ribbon, and the ribbon is stronger than any bond to a coated paper surface.

Two smaller things about the ribbon are worth recording. The point where it exits the sleeve is a stress concentration in the wrap, and it tore at the slot corners on the first attempt until a reinforcement patch was added behind it. And the free end has to live somewhere when the drawer is closed: anything that sits in the gap between the tray and the sleeve will eventually end up jammed in the slide, which is a warranty problem rather than an aesthetic one.

The failure that only appears in a container

The second sample glided perfectly. The third — the first one that went by sea — bound, on roughly one unit in twelve, in the last 30 mm of travel.

The cause was not the clearance getting smaller. It was the board getting bigger. Paperboard takes up moisture and swells, and it swells appreciably more across the grain than with it. At 0.5 mm per side, which was the clearance on the second sample, humidity alone is enough to close the gap; a month in a container at elevated relative humidity is enough to close it on the units whose grain happened to run the wrong way.

Two changes fixed it together. The clearance was opened to 0.8 mm per side, and the wrap was specified with a matte lamination — chosen for the print finish, but it also seals the board against moisture uptake and lowers the sliding friction on both mating faces. The tray now glides more easily than the unlaminated version did, at a clearance that survives the crossing.

Structure and specification

  • Structure: two-part drawer box — wrapped sleeve with a closed back, wrapped tray, ribbon pull through the sleeve front
  • Board: 2.0 mm greyboard in the sleeve, 1.5 mm in the tray. The sleeve is the larger panel and the one that takes the pallet load; the thinner tray board buys interior volume, and interior volume is exactly what the cushion needs.
  • Wrap: 157 g coated paper, printed CMYK with a matte lamination, single-colour interior print. The lamination is doing three jobs here — print finish, moisture seal and sliding friction.
  • Interior: die-cut EVA cushion wrapped in a suede-feel fabric, seated in a cavity cut 2 mm undersize on each side for an interference fit
  • Clearance: 0.8 mm per side on width and height, taken out of the sleeve interior
  • Ribbon: 15 mm grosgrain, passed through a slot in the tray board and trapped under the wrap, exiting through a reinforced slot in the sleeve front
  • Travel stop: a paperboard tab glued inside the sleeve at the rear. A tray that pulls all the way out and drops is a real failure, and it is cheaper to prevent with a tab than with a longer ribbon.
  • Finishing: foil stamping on the sleeve top panel only — deliberately not on the tray's exterior faces. Foil is a hard, raised layer; on a sliding surface it raises friction and it polishes.

The assembly step that decides whether the drawer glides

Two things in the packing sequence matter more than anything in the drawing.

Strap the watch to the cushion outside the box, then seat the cushion. Doing it the other way round means buckling a strap inside a 60 mm deep tray, which is a two-hand job in a confined space and the reason the strap gets scuffed.

Check the sleeve interior with a gauge, not by eye. The defect that causes binding is not a sleeve that is too small overall — it is a sleeve whose corners were formed slightly proud, so the interior is correct in the middle of each panel and tight at the four corners, which is exactly where the tray's corners travel. A go/no-go gauge cut to the minimum interior dimension catches it in a second. The alternative is discovering it as an intermittent bind that no amount of specification review will explain.

What to take from it

  • Take the slide clearance out of the sleeve, never out of the tray. The tray's interior is the cushion's, and the cushion is what holds the product. Give the sleeve the clearance and let the product keep its dimensions.
  • Make the fitment fit tighter than the slide. Otherwise the drawer comes out and the product stays where it was. On a compression fitment that means cutting the cavity undersize, not to size.
  • Anchor a ribbon through the board, under the wrap. A glued ribbon is an adhesive failure waiting for a customer who pulls harder than expected.
  • Budget clearance for humidity, not for the factory. Paperboard swells across the grain, and 0.5 mm a side is inside that range. A laminated wrap seals it and lowers friction at the same time.
  • Keep foil and heavy finishes off the sliding faces. They raise friction and they polish where the tray rubs.
  • Gauge the sleeve interior. The binding defect lives at the corners, where nothing but a gauge will find it.

The box that shipped is not a clever structure. It is a standard drawer box in which one dimension — the clearance — was taken from the right place, and one component — the ribbon — was anchored to the board instead of to the paper. Both are decisions that cost nothing at the drawing stage and neither is a decision that can be made properly after the first sample exists.
