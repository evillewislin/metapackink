The brief was a three-piece skincare gift set in a magnetic closure rigid box, to be sold as a seasonal retail item and shipped direct to customers as well. Nothing in the brief was unusual. The project is worth writing up because of the decision that shaped it: the insert, which was specified last and ended up determining the box size, the freight volume per unit and the number of assembly steps.

Names, artwork and commercial figures are left out. Dimensions, board, wrap, insert material, finishing and quantity band are not, because those are the parts a buyer running a similar project can reuse.

The set being boxed

  • A 50 ml glass serum bottle with a pump and a separate cap, 118 mm tall including the pump
  • A 30 ml glass treatment bottle with a dropper, 96 mm tall
  • A 15 ml glass eye cream jar, 55 mm tall

Three glass containers, three different heights, three different diameters, and a combined weight of roughly 420 g once filled. The tallest and the shortest differ by more than a factor of two, which is the fact that drove everything else.

The obvious answer, and why it was wrong

The first proposal was a single flat tray with three cutout cavities, die-cut from paperboard and folded into a shallow box. It is the standard solution for a presentation set: cheap, light, and it holds the containers in a fixed arrangement.

It was rejected on the third container. A die-cut paperboard cavity locates a container by surrounding it, and it holds the shape by being folded into a three-dimensional form. With one tall container immediately beside one short container, the folded rib between them has to span the full height of the taller one to stay rigid. That rib consumed more clearance than the containers themselves, and the arrangement became loose — the tall bottle could lean, and a leaning pump in a closed box is a leak waiting for a courier.

The second attempt tried a two-tier arrangement to shorten the ribs. That worked structurally but made the box taller, which made the freight cost worse, and it introduced a lift-out tray that had to be repacked at assembly.

The decision: a moulded insert, and the box grew

The set was built on a moulded pulp insert with three cavities of different depths, each sized to its own container.

This is where the project became instructive. A moulded insert holds its shape through its own wall thickness. That thickness is not free: it is subtracted from the usable interior of the box on every side. Across three cavities plus the outer wall, the cavity structure was roughly 10 mm thicker on each side than the paperboard tray it replaced.

Nothing about the containers had changed. The box grew by around 20 mm in both the length and the width dimensions, because the insert around them grew.

That has a cost that is easy to miss on a sample and hard to miss on a shipping invoice. For a box of this size, 20 mm on two dimensions is on the order of a fifteen per cent increase in exterior volume. Since finished packaging is charged on volumetric weight once it is packed in export cartons, the insert choice moved the freight cost per unit — and it did so at the point in the project where the box dimensions were already locked into the artwork.

What the insert bought in return

The extra volume was not wasted. Three things were gained that the paperboard tray could not deliver:

  • Each container is held at its own depth. The eye cream jar sits in a shallow cavity rather than a cavity sized for the serum bottle, so the three tops sit level in the box regardless of container height. Level tops are what make a presentation set look designed rather than assembled.
  • The pump is protected from the lid. The tallest cavity is recessed below the top edge of the insert, leaving clearance between the pump and the inside of the lid. In a flat tray the pump sits proud and the lid closes onto it.
  • Assembly got simpler, not harder. Containers drop into moulded cavities in one motion. The paperboard version needed the ribs to be held open while each container was seated, which is a two-hand operation on a line that is otherwise one-hand.

Structure and specification

  • Structure: magnetic closure rigid box, lid with a 22 mm skirt, base 45 mm deep
  • Board: 2.0 mm greyboard throughout. Chosen against the 420 g loaded weight and the 215 mm box length — a longer panel of 1.5 mm board would have flexed under the stacked weight in a carton.
  • Wrap: 157 g coated paper, printed CMYK with a matte lamination. Matte was specified over soft-touch because the box is expected to be handled and put away rather than opened once; soft-touch marks permanently under handling.
  • Insert: moulded pulp, three cavities at 122 mm, 100 mm and 60 mm depth, unbleached.
  • Closure: two magnets in the skirt, positioned against the lid overhang width. This is worth a note: a lid of this width carried by the base will lift at the corners if the magnets are sized by habit rather than by the overhang. Two was the minimum that held the corners down under a base-only carry.
  • Finishing: foil stamping on the front panel only, and debossing on the lid edge. Both were kept off the textured areas — the wrap had a light texture and foil adhesion on it was unreliable, which was established at sampling rather than discovered in production.
  • Interior printing: a single-colour repeat pattern on the wrap's interior face. Cost is almost entirely in the printing plate, which is why an interior pattern is close to free once the wrap is being printed anyway, and it is where most of the unboxing impression came from.

What went wrong, and what it cost

Two things, both worth recording because both were avoidable at the brief stage.

The box size was set before the insert was chosen. Artwork for the wrap was already in progress when the paperboard tray was rejected. Enlarging the box by 20 mm meant the artwork had to be re-laid against a new dieline. Nothing was printed, so the cost was time rather than money, but on a seasonal item the schedule was the constraint that mattered.

The magnet count was specified from a previous project. The first sample closed convincingly when pressed shut on a table and lifted at the front corners when carried by the base. This is exactly the check that a photograph cannot perform, and it is why the sample was approved loaded, closed, inverted and carried rather than looked at.

What to take from it

  • Choose the insert before the box dimensions, not after. The insert sets the interior; the interior sets the exterior; the exterior sets the freight. Working in that order costs nothing and saves a re-lay.
  • Moulded inserts are worth their volume when heights differ. With containers of similar height, a die-cut paperboard tray is lighter, cheaper and thinner. The advantage reverses as soon as one container is significantly taller than another.
  • Specify magnets against lid overhang, and test by carrying the box by the base. Two checks that take ten seconds on the sample and prevent a whole shipment of boxes whose corners lift.
  • Confirm finish adhesion on the actual stock at sampling. Foil on a textured wrap was the one combination that could have failed in production, and it was caught because the sample was tested rather than inspected.
  • If the box will ship in export cartons, treat exterior volume as a cost line. Twenty millimetres on two dimensions is not a rounding error.

The finished set met the brief and the structure was sound. The lesson is smaller and more useful than that: on a multi-component set, the sequence in which the decisions are made is itself a design decision, and it is cheaper to get it right than to get it fast.
