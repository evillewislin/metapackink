A magnetic closure box has one requirement a lid-and-base box does not: it has to stay shut until somebody decides to open it, and then open without a fight. Both halves of that sentence are set by the same four variables — magnet grade, magnet size, how many there are, and where they sit — and in most briefs all four are copied from the last box the brand made rather than chosen for this one.

The two failure modes are symmetrical and easy to recognise once you know what to look at. A closure that is too weak lets the lid peel open in transit, or lifts at the corners the moment a customer picks the box up by the base. A closure that is too strong makes the box impossible to open one-handed, marks the wrap along the closure edge, and eventually tears the wrap away from the board it is glued to, because the customer is pulling harder than the structure was designed to give. Neither is really a magnet problem. Both are the same specification error in opposite directions.

What the magnet is actually holding shut

It is worth being precise about the load, because it is much smaller than most people assume. A rigid box lid of this size weighs somewhere between 60 and 120 g. The magnet is not holding back a door; it is resisting four much lighter things:

  • The lid's own weight, when the box is turned on edge or carried at an angle.
  • The product pressing outward against the inside of the lid. A box packed to its full depth pushes, and a set with a tall pump or cap pushing directly on the lid panel is a bigger load than the lid itself.
  • Vibration in transit, which is a cyclic load rather than a static one. This is what walks a marginal closure open over a long journey when the same closure survives a static inversion test.
  • The base-only carry. A customer picks the box up by the base and the entire weight of the contents is trying to separate the lid from the base at the front edge — exactly where the magnet is.

The last one is usually what decides the specification, because the load and the holding force are applied at the same place. It is also the check that a photograph can never perform, which is why a magnetic sample has to be approved by carrying it rather than by looking at it.

Magnet grade: what N42 tells you, and what it does not

Packaging magnets are neodymium, and the grade — N35, N38, N42, N45, N52 — is the maximum energy product of the material. A higher number means more force from the same volume of magnet.

For a rigid box, N38 to N42 covers nearly every case. The jump to N52 costs real money and buys force that nothing in the box needs. It is also the last variable worth reaching for: the difference between N42 and N52 on a thin disc is far smaller than the difference half a millimetre of separation makes, and higher grades are marginally more brittle, which matters when the magnet is a 2 mm thick disc being pressed into a board recess.

Grade is the thing most often over-specified and the thing least often worth specifying. Geometry and gap come first, and both are decided in the dieline rather than on the magnet supplier's price list.

Size, count, and why two usually beat one

Pull force at contact scales with the area of the pole face, not with the thickness of the magnet. A wide, thin disc gives more hold in less space than a narrow, thick one — which matters, because the space available is the thickness of the skirt board.

Count is the more interesting decision. Two magnets placed out towards the corners of the front skirt hold a lid down better than one magnet of the same total area placed centrally, because the failure being resisted is not a straight pull but a rotation: the lid hinges at the back and lifts at the front corners. Two magnets spaced apart give that rotation a longer lever arm to fight against.

The practical rule we work from is that the magnet count follows the width of the lid. A narrow box — under about 150 mm — will usually hold on a single central magnet. Past that, and on any box with a deep lid skirt, two spaced magnets is the specification, and a lid approaching 300 mm with a heavy wrapped finish is where a third is worth quoting.

The air gap: the millimetre that costs the most

Magnet-to-steel force falls away very quickly with separation, and separation is not air — it is everything between the two halves. The wrap over the magnet, the adhesive, the board the magnet is recessed into, and the wrap on the facing panel all count.

This is why the magnets are recessed into the front skirt of the base rather than buried inside the wall, and why the steel counterpart is recessed into the lid skirt to match: the goal is to leave as little material between them as the structure allows. On a well-built closure that is the wrap on each side and nothing else — a fraction of a millimetre in total.

The rule of thumb is blunt but useful: every additional half-millimetre of material between the magnet and its counterpart costs more holding force than moving up two grades gains. A box whose magnets were recessed 2 mm too deep in the first sample will not be fixed by specifying a stronger magnet. It is fixed in the dieline.

Position: where in the skirt, and against what

Four things about position are worth settling before the sample is made.

Which face. Magnets sit in the vertical skirt at the front of the box, not in the top or bottom panel. A magnet in the top panel is being asked to resist shear rather than pull, and shear is the direction a magnetic closure is worst at.

How far from the edge. Too close to the outer face and there is not enough wrap over the magnet, so it telegraphs as a ridge under the paper and can eventually push through it. Too far in and the lever arm on the lid corner grows. Centred in the height of the skirt, inset from the side edges, is the usual answer.

What it closes against. A mild steel plate is the default: it is cheaper, it has no polarity to get wrong, and the magnet will hold anywhere on it. A second magnet roughly doubles the force available in the same space and self-locates, but the polarity has to be right on every unit and it is the single part most likely to be assembled wrong on a line. We quote steel unless there is a reason not to.

Registration. The two halves have to meet. If the lid sits proud, or the lid and base skirts are different heights, the magnets meet off-centre and the effective pull drops well below what either magnet is rated for. This is a dieline dimension, and on a box where the lid overhangs the base it is the dimension most often wrong on the first sample.

Lid overhang and the lever you are actually fighting

The lid is hinged at the back and held at the front, so the closure is resisting a rotation about the back edge. Everything that makes the lid heavier or deeper increases that rotation and therefore the force required:

  • Lid footprint. A wider lid has more panel and more wrap, and its weight acts further from the hinge.
  • Skirt depth. A deep skirt moves the magnet further from the hinge line and adds board and wrap weight at the same time.
  • Finishing on the lid. Foil, a heavy emboss or a wrapped rigid fitment on the lid all add grams at the worst possible distance from the pivot.

One thing works in the opposite direction and is worth knowing: a lid that fits tightly on the base will hold itself shut by friction and make a weak magnet set feel adequate on a table. The same box will lift its corners the first time somebody carries it by the base. Friction is not a closure, and a sample approved on a tabletop has not tested the closure at all.

What shut should feel like, and how to test it

The target is a definite arrival rather than a slam. If you have to push the lid down, either the magnets are too strong or they are closing across too small a gap — and the customer will pay for it at the other end, in a box that has to be put down on a table to be opened.

Three tests, in this order, on a sample loaded with the real product:

  1. Close it, turn it upside down and hold it there. The lid should not move. This is the minimum, and it is the only one of the three that a static specification would catch.
  2. Carry it by the base, one hand, and watch the front corners of the lid. This is the check that catches an undersized closure almost every time, because it is the load the box will actually meet.
  3. Open it one-handed. If that is not comfortable, the closure is too strong. On a premium box this is the failure that damages the brand more than a lid that lifts.

If the box ships direct to customers rather than through retail, add a fourth: drop the loaded, closed box onto a corner from about 150 mm, twice. A closure that survives inversion can still walk open under vibration, and ten seconds on a sample is cheaper than a return rate.

What to write into the brief

Most buyers do not need to name a grade. What they need to specify is the performance, because performance is what a factory can engineer against and a grade is not:

  • the box must hold shut when inverted and when carried by the base;
  • it must open one-handed without the box having to be put down;
  • it must do both after a drop test, if the box ships direct.

Given that, the structure, the board, the lid dimensions and the wrap are enough for a factory to choose grade, size, count and position — and to prove it on a sample. If you do want the parts specified, write down the grade, the magnet dimensions, the count, the position measured from the side edges and from the top of the skirt, and what the counterpart is. What you should not write down is a position copied from a different box, because the skirt depth and the lid overhang will almost certainly differ.

The checks, in order

  • Approve the closure loaded, not empty. A product pressing on the lid changes the load, and an empty box tells you nothing about it.
  • Test by carrying the box by the base. It is the load the box will actually meet, and it catches undersized magnets that pass every static test.
  • Test opening one-handed. A closure that is too strong is a defect, not a sign of quality.
  • Check that the magnets are recessed, not buried. Look at the sample's closure edge: if you can feel the magnet as a ridge, there is too little wrap over it.
  • Confirm registration on the sample. The lid should meet the base flush, with the skirt heights matched, or the magnets are not meeting square.
  • Do not let friction stand in for holding force. A tight lid makes a weak closure feel fine until the box is lifted.
  • Re-check the closure if the lid changes. A deeper lid, a heavier finish or a bigger footprint all change the load, and the magnet specification has to move with them.

None of this needs a buyer to become a magnet engineer. It needs a decision about how the box should feel in the hand, made before the dieline is drawn rather than after the sample arrives — because by then the things that would have to change are the board recesses and the skirt dimensions, and those are the parts of a rigid box that are least willing to move.
