Perfume packaging has one structural problem that most other categories get to ignore. The product is the heaviest thing in the box by a wide margin, it is made of the most fragile common material, and it is tall relative to the footprint it stands on. A filled 100 ml bottle is around 430 g of glass balanced on a base smaller than a coaster. Every decision in the structure — the cradle, the board, the lid depth, the drop test — is downstream of that one sentence.

Most perfume boxes are designed in the opposite order. The box is drawn as a pleasing volume, the bottle is dropped into it, and an insert is cut to fill whatever space is left. When the bottle weighs 430 g and its neck is eight millimetres of glass, that order produces a box that photographs perfectly and fails in a courier network. This is the order we use instead, and the reason for each step.

Start with the weight, not the volume

The numbers are worth having before anything is drawn. Glass is about 2.5 g/cm³, and a perfume bottle is thick-walled because it is moulded and because weight reads as quality. A 50 ml bottle comes in at roughly 250 g filled; a 100 ml bottle at 420 to 450 g. Those are not cosmetic weights. They are the weight of a hardback book standing on a 50 mm square.

Static load is not the problem — 430 g resting on a rigid base floor is nothing. The problem is the dynamic case, because a parcel is dropped, and a dropped bottle does not weigh 430 g for the twenty milliseconds that matter. A 100 ml bottle in a box dropped from about a metre onto one corner puts a peak load into the base several times its own weight, concentrated on whatever is under the bottle at the moment of impact.

Two specification decisions follow, and they are the ones that separate a perfume box that survives from one that does not:

  • The lid and the base are not the same board. The lid carries nothing except its own panel. On a 100 ml box we specify 2.0 mm greyboard on the base and 1.5 mm on the lid, and on a 50 ml box 1.5 mm throughout. Specifying 2.0 mm on both adds material cost and exterior volume to the one part with no load in it.
  • The base floor gets a doubler. A second layer of board glued under the cradle's footprint, inside the base, turning the panel the bottle actually loads from 1.5 mm into 3 mm. It costs a few cents, it takes about 1.5 mm of interior height, and it is the single cheapest thing that stops a base splitting at the crease on a corner drop.

The load path to check is the one people assume rather than trace. The bottle stands on the cradle, the cradle stands on the base floor, the base floor is glued to the base walls, and the base walls are what the customer's hand is holding. It is a short path with one weak link, and the weak link is the glued corner of the base, not the board thickness.

The cradle: hold the body, never the neck

Glass is strong in compression along its axis and useless against a point load applied across it. A bottle standing upright, loaded straight down through its base, will take a great deal. The same bottle loaded sideways at the neck will not. So the cradle's job is to grip the body and to leave the neck alone, and there are three ways of getting that wrong.

Not enough contact height. A cradle that touches the bottle over ten millimetres of its height is a locator, not a holder: the bottle can pivot inside it. We work to a minimum of 25 to 30 mm of contact along the body, which on a 100 ml bottle is comfortably available and on a squat 30 ml bottle sometimes is not — and when it is not, the answer is a taller cradle rather than a tighter one.

Cutting the cavity to the bottle's widest point. This is the most common error and it only shows up on tapered bottles. A bottle that narrows towards the shoulder will sink through a cavity cut to its widest dimension until something stops it, and it then sits lower than designed, unsupported at the height the cradle was meant to grip. The cavity is cut to the bottle's profile at the height it is meant to sit, which is not the same drawing.

Round bottle, straight-sided cavity. A cylinder inside a rectangular cutout touches the four faces at their midpoints and leaves a void at each corner. It is held, and it looks loose. If the cavity is visible when the lid comes off — and in a perfume box it usually is — cut a circular or radiused cavity instead, because the appearance of a snug fit is part of what the box is selling.

The shoulder is the other place to keep clear. A cradle whose top edge rises past the bottle's shoulder is gripping a curved, thinning section of glass that was never meant to take a side load. Keep the top edge below the shoulder, and let the neck and cap sit in free air.

Cap clearance, the dimension nobody draws

Caps are larger than people expect before they measure one. On a great many perfume bottles the cap is 30 to 40 per cent of the bottle's total height, wider than the body on some designs, and finished with the logo — a foil block, a lacquered disc, a metal badge. It is the most decorated and most fragile part of the product, and it is the part most often designed into contact with something.

Two clearances have to be drawn, and both are usually left to the sampling stage to discover:

  • Radial: 1.0 to 1.5 mm around the cap. Not zero. A cap that touches the cavity wall on one side is a cap that is being pushed, and on a friction-fit cap that push is what works it loose in transit.
  • Vertical: 4 to 6 mm between the top of the cap and the lid's interior face. This one is not about impact. A lid on a two-piece box slides down over the base's skirt, and if the cap is within a millimetre or two of the lid interior, the lid catches the cap on the way down — it tilts, it marks the foil, and the customer meets that the first time they close the box rather than the first time they open it.

There is a structural temptation here worth naming. A recess cut to accept the cap, so the cap is held from above as well as the body from below, looks like a more complete solution and is a worse one. It puts the load path through the cap and then through the neck joint, which is the weakest glass section on the bottle, and on a friction cap it is also a leak waiting to happen. Restrain the body. Leave the cap alone.

The neck: what a collar is for, and when it is the wrong answer

A collar — a board frame set into the top of the base, surrounding the neck — is the standard way to stop a bottle leaning. It is also the part most often specified as though it were a grip, which it should never be.

The distinction that matters is between restraining and retaining. A collar that fits the neck closely is retaining it: the neck is loaded every time the box is handled, and the neck is the one place glass cannot take that. A collar cut with about 1 mm of clearance all round is restraining it — it does nothing until the bottle has already moved, and then it stops the movement. Same part, opposite specification, and the difference is a millimetre.

Where a collar earns its place is on a tall bottle in a shallow box, or on a coffret where the bottles lean towards each other. Where it is unnecessary is a 50 ml bottle in a box proportioned to it, sitting in a cradle with 25 mm of contact height. In that case the collar is a part, an operation and a place for the wrap to wrinkle, buying a constraint the cradle already provides.

Insert material: the constraint unique to fragrance

Foam, board and moulded pulp all appear in perfume boxes, and the choice is usually argued on cost and on finish. There is a fourth criterion in this category that outranks both.

The insert has to be odour-neutral. A box that opens with a chemical smell is a defect in perfume more than in any other category, because the customer's first act on opening it is to smell the contents. EVA is fine once it is fully cured and aired, and we specify low-VOC grade and hold it before assembly for that reason. Some polyurethane foams and some recycled-content foams are not fine, and the smell is not something a sample check three weeks after production will catch.

Against that, board is the safer material and it is more recyclable: a paperboard cradle goes into the same stream as the carton, and it can be printed, wrapped or foil-blocked to match the box. What board cannot do is grip. It has almost no compliance, so it locates rather than grips, and it cannot be undercut to trap anything. For a bottle standing under its own weight in a snug cavity, that is enough. For a bottle that has to be held against a side load, it is not.

Moulded pulp is rarely right here. Its surface is coarser than a polished bottle deserves, it sheds fibres, and it holds moisture — which next to glass is the beginning of a staining problem.

One more trade-off is worth stating because it decides a surprising number of these projects. Bottles seep. A crimped or poorly seated spray neck will weep a millilitre or two over a long transit, and board absorbs that and keeps the smell permanently, while foam does not. If the bottle has a screw neck, or a pour neck, or any history of seepage, that argues for foam despite its recyclability cost. If the closure is a crimped spray and the brief is about finish and end-of-life, board wins. It is a product decision before it is a packaging one.

When one box becomes a coffret

Add a second bottle — a 100 ml with a 10 ml travel spray, or a bottle with a refill — and two things change at once.

The weight roughly doubles, and somewhere around 800 g of total contents the box stops being a container and becomes a thing that is picked up and carried. The base floor takes the whole of it, the glued base corners take the whole of it, and the customer is now lifting it by the base. That is the point at which the doubler stops being optional and 2.5 mm board on the base enters the conversation. It is also the point at which the brief usually asks for a handle, and the honest answer is that a handle puts the entire load into one panel of board — a wider base and a slightly shorter box carry better and cost less.

The second change is that the range now has a tallest item and a heaviest item, and they are usually different ones. The tallest sets the lid depth, the heaviest sets the board, and the coffret has to be dimensioned against both. A divider keeps glass off glass, which matters more than it sounds: two bottles touching inside a box that is being vibrated for six weeks will mark each other.

Drop testing a perfume box

Perfume boxes get tested empty, or tested with a representative bottle, and both miss the failure. Test the heaviest bottle in the range, in the finished box, with the full insert — the worst case is not the average one.

Four orientations, from roughly 800 mm to a metre for a parcel under 2 kg: flat on the base, flat on the lid, on a long side, and one corner. The corner drop is the one that finds things, and it is the one that gets skipped because the box will not balance on a corner. Hold it.

What actually fails, in the order we see it:

  • The cradle tears. A foam cavity cut close to the edge of the pad splits at the cut under a corner drop, and the bottle then rides down into the tear and sits crooked. More material around the cavity, not a tighter cavity.
  • The base floor splits at the crease. This is the doubler's job.
  • The lid comes off. A friction-held lid walks off under vibration, and a 430 g bottle in a lidless box is then exposed on three sides. A rigid box is not a shipping container: either an outer shipper, or at minimum a paper band.

The checks, in order

  • Weigh the filled bottle before anything is drawn. Board, doubler and drop height all follow from that number, and it is almost always higher than the brief assumed.
  • Specify the base board heavier than the lid board. The lid carries nothing. Putting 2.0 mm on both adds cost and exterior volume to the part with no load in it.
  • Cut the cradle to the bottle's profile at the height it sits, not to its widest point. A tapered bottle sinks through a cavity cut to its widest dimension and ends up unsupported.
  • Keep 25 to 30 mm of contact height on the body, and the cradle's top edge below the shoulder. Contact height is what stops a bottle pivoting; the shoulder is the one place it cannot take a side load.
  • Draw both cap clearances: 1.0 to 1.5 mm radial, 4 to 6 mm vertical. The vertical one is what stops the lid catching the cap on the way down and marking the foil.
  • Restrain the neck, never retain it. A collar gets about a millimetre of clearance so it only acts after the bottle has moved.
  • Choose the insert on odour first. A box that smells is a defect in this category before it is anything else.
  • Drop the heaviest bottle, on a corner, in the finished box. The corner is the orientation that finds a torn cradle and a split base.
  • Plan the outer. A friction-held lid walks off, and a bottle in a lidless box has nothing left protecting it.

None of this requires a buyer to learn structural engineering. It requires knowing that in this category the bottle is not something inside the box, it is the load the box exists to carry — and that the two dimensions worth arguing about at the sampling stage are the two a photograph cannot show: how much of the body the cradle actually touches, and how much air there is above the cap.
