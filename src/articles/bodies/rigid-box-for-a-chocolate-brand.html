The brief was a presentation box for a range of single-origin chocolate bars, to sit on a shelf, in a shop window, and to ship direct to customers. Nothing about the box was unusual. The project is worth writing up because of the product: tempered chocolate stops being solid well below the temperature a shop window reaches, it takes on the smell of whatever it is packed against, and it never recovers from either. Every decision below follows from those two facts.

Names, artwork and commercial figures are left out. Dimensions, board, wrap, insert material and finishing are not, because those are the parts a buyer with a similar product can reuse.

The product being boxed

  • Four bars, 70 g each, 110 × 55 × 9 mm, tempered dark and milk chocolate
  • Each bar in foil, then a printed paper sleeve, then the box
  • Melting range finishing around 32 °C, and a snap that is already going above about 28 °C
  • Retail shelf and window display, plus direct-to-customer parcels, in a climate where a summer afternoon reaches 38 °C

A chocolate bar is a thin slab of a material that is solid at 25 °C and plastic at 32 °C. That is a narrower window than most packaging engineers ever have to work inside, and it is the window that decided the structure.

Three failure modes, none of them breakage

Chocolate does not arrive broken. A bar that chips is still saleable; a bar that has been warm is not. The three ways it actually fails, in the order they matter:

  • Deformation. Above roughly 28 °C a tempered bar loses its snap and becomes plastic. Held in any orientation other than flat and fully supported, it sags under its own weight — and when it cools it sets in that shape. A bar that arrived bent is a bar that looks wrong on the plate even though it tastes identical.
  • Bloom. Warm the bar and let fat migrate to the surface, then cool it, and cocoa butter recrystallises there as a grey haze. Warm it in humid air and sugar dissolves and recrystallises as a gritty white film. Both are permanent, both are cosmetic, and both read to a customer as old chocolate.
  • Odour. Cocoa butter is a fat, and fats absorb volatile compounds. A bar stored in a box that smells of adhesive or foam tastes of it within days. This is the failure mode that is entirely the packaging's fault and the one most often discovered after production.

Notice what is not on that list: impact. A rigid box protects against impact whether or not you think about it. None of the three above are fixed by cushioning, which is why the first sample — perfectly cushioned — was wrong.

The first sample: a foam cavity per bar

The obvious answer was a die-cut EVA insert with four bar-shaped cavities, each bar held in its own recess. It is the standard presentation solution, it holds everything still, and it protects the bars from impact better than anything else.

It failed on two counts, both of which showed up within a day of the sample arriving.

It gripped the bars. A cavity cut to the bar profile holds it by contact on both faces. That is fine at 25 °C. At 34 °C the bar is plastic, and a plastic bar in a cavity that is gripping it takes the shape of the cavity and then sets that way. The failure is not visible in the box — it is visible when the customer unwraps a bar that is no longer flat.

It smelled. Closed empty for twenty-four hours at 40 °C, the sample box had a distinct adhesive and foam note on opening. That is the test for this category and the first sample failed it. Chocolate does not need much: cocoa butter will pick up volatiles at levels a person cannot smell in the room but can taste in the fat.

A third problem was going to arrive later and was avoided by the same change. A softened bar sags into whatever supports it, and a foam cavity supports it everywhere, which means a bar that sags in foam sets permanently curved. A flat surface does at least let it set flat.

The decision: a flat platform, one layer, and six millimetres of air

The insert that shipped is a die-cut paperboard platform with four shallow wells, standing 6 mm off the base floor on folded flaps, with a low divider between each pair of bars.

Three things about it, and the third is the one that matters:

  • The bars lie flat, in a single layer. No bar carries the weight of another. At 34 °C, 70 g resting across the middle of a bar is enough to bow it, and a bowed bar sets bowed.
  • Nothing grips anything. Every well is cut 1.5 mm oversize on length and width, with the divider 2 mm lower than the bar. The bars are located, not held. A bar that softens is free to stay flat rather than being held into a shape.
  • There is 6 mm of still air on five of the six faces. The standoff under the platform, the perimeter gap between the platform and the base walls, and the space above the bars to the rim. That gap is the insulation, and it is the thing that actually keeps the chocolate solid for the three hours a shop window is in the sun.

The box grew. A single layer of four bars is wider and shallower than two layers of two, and the exterior went from 180 × 130 × 52 mm to 246 × 132 × 46 mm. Wider and shallower is the better outcome for a shelf — it presents the bars rather than stacking them — and it was not a freight problem, because the box was already being shipped flat-ish in retail cartons rather than assembled. It was also, as it turned out, the reason the thermal problem was solvable at all.

Why the air gap is the insulation

Packaging people reach for insulation as a material, and in a box it is mostly a geometry. Still air conducts at roughly 0.026 W/m·K; paperboard is around 0.05 to 0.07; and a direct contact path between two solid surfaces conducts orders of magnitude better than either, because there is no interface resistance in it.

What that means in practice: the heat that reaches the chocolate travels through whatever the chocolate is touching, not through the air around it. A bar lying directly on a board panel that is glued to the base floor has a conduction path from the outer wrap straight into itself. The same bar on a platform standing 6 mm above that panel, with a folded flap at each corner and nothing else, has a 6 mm air layer and four small board bridges. The bridges are the reason the standoff is folded flaps rather than a solid rim.

Foam would have insulated better per millimetre and was still the wrong answer, because the failure it was protecting against was not the one that was going to happen. Insulation buys time. Deformation and odour are what the product actually did.

One honest limit, stated up front to the brand: no box keeps chocolate solid in a window that reaches 45 °C indefinitely. What the air gap buys is roughly two to three hours of margin over a box with the bars in contact with the board, which is the difference between a bar that survives an afternoon and one that does not.

The wrap colour, measured rather than argued

The brand wanted matte black. It is the right choice for the shelf and the wrong one for the window, and rather than argue it we made two samples — identical structure, one in matte black wrap and one in a warm stone — and left both in a south-facing window in July.

After three hours: the black wrap measured 61 °C on its exterior surface and the stone wrap 45 °C. Interior air was 41 °C against 33 °C. Sixteen degrees at the surface and eight degrees inside, from nothing but the colour of the paper.

The bars in the black box had softened. The bars in the stone box had not. The brand kept matte black, which was their decision to make and a defensible one — the shelf is where most units are sold and the window is one display in one shop. What changed as a result of the measurement was the display guidance in the retail pack and a recommendation that the box not be the item left in direct sun. That is the whole value of measuring it: the decision was made with the number in front of everyone rather than discovered in August.

Odour: the test nobody runs

If there is one thing to take from this project it is the empty-box test, because it is free and it is the only check that catches the third failure mode before production.

Close the finished, empty box. Leave it for twenty-four hours at 40 °C. Open it and smell the interior. A box that has a smell will give that smell to the chocolate, and there is no way to detect it from a specification or a photograph.

Three things were specified as a result:

  • Water-based adhesive, fully cured. Solvent-based adhesives off-gas for days. Water-based ones still off-gas while curing, so the cure matters as much as the chemistry.
  • No foam anywhere in the assembly. Closed-cell EVA is the usual suspect. It is also the reason the platform is folded board with no laminated foam pad under it.
  • UV-cured ink held before assembly. A UV-cured print that goes straight from the press into a box carries a sharp note that dissipates in a day or two on a shelf and not at all in a sealed box. The wrap was printed, aired, and then assembled.

Bloom, and what the foil is for

Fat bloom is a heat-cycling problem and nothing in the box prevents it once the chocolate has been warm. What the box can do is slow the cycling: the air gap that keeps the bar solid is the same air gap that flattens the temperature swing, because it slows heat transfer in both directions.

Sugar bloom is a moisture problem, and it is not the box's job. A bar in foil has a moisture barrier; the box does not need to be one, and specifying a board with a moisture-resistant coating would have been spending money to duplicate the foil. The one thing the box does contribute is that it is made of a hygroscopic material: paperboard takes up and releases water with the room, so a box stored in a humid warehouse arrives with a slightly damp interior and the foil inside it is what keeps that away from the chocolate. Keep the bars in the foil. That is the specification.

Structure and specification

  • Structure: two-piece rigid box, lid with a 22 mm skirt, base interior 22 mm deep, exterior 246 × 132 × 46 mm
  • Board: 1.5 mm greyboard throughout. The product weighs 280 g across four bars and nothing is stacked on it in a carton — a heavier board would have added exterior volume and, because the base floor is a conduction surface rather than a load surface, no thermal benefit.
  • Wrap: 157 g coated paper, matte lamination, black outside and unprinted inside. The unprinted interior was deliberate: it is the surface the bars sit against and the fewer inks and coatings in that path, the fewer volatiles there are to give away.
  • Insert: die-cut paperboard platform with four wells, standing 6 mm above the base floor on four folded flaps, wells cut 1.5 mm oversize, dividers 2 mm below the bar height. One steel-rule die, no mould.
  • Adhesive: water-based, cured before assembly.
  • Finishing: blind deboss on the lid, no foil. Foil was in the first artwork and was dropped — a metalised area on the panel the sun reaches is a warm spot on the surface that sets the interior temperature, and a deboss reads better on a matte black wrap anyway.

What went wrong, and what it cost

Two things, both caught on the sample and both structural rather than cosmetic.

The stacked layout. The original drawing put the bars in two layers of two, which is the layout that fits the smallest box. It was rejected after the thermal reasoning rather than before it: at 34 °C the top row loads the bottom row at the two points where the bars overlap, and a bar loaded at two points sags between them. Going to a single layer made the box 66 mm wider and 6 mm shallower. That cost a re-lay of the artwork and nothing else, and it is the change that made the platform work.

The first foam sample. Quoted and made, and rejected on odour and on grip. It cost a second sampling round, and it would have cost a great deal more if the empty-box test had not been run on it — a box that smells is a defect that appears after the run is paid for, in the form of reviews rather than claims.

What to take from it

  • Identify the failure mode before choosing the material. Chocolate fails by heat, bloom and odour. Foam solves impact, which was never the problem.
  • Never grip a product that softens. Locate it with clearance, support it across its whole area, and let it set in the shape it was made in.
  • Insulation in a box is a gap, not a material. Six millimetres of still air and four small bridges beats a solid contact path, and it costs nothing but exterior volume.
  • One layer, always. A warm product carrying the weight of another warm product deforms, and it sets deformed.
  • Run the empty-box test. Twenty-four hours closed at 40 °C, then open and smell. It is the only check that catches odour transfer before production.
  • Measure the wrap colour if the box will sit in the sun. Sixteen degrees at the surface from paper choice alone. Whether the brand takes the number or not, they should have it.
  • Keep the product in its own barrier. The foil is the moisture barrier. Do not ask the board to be one.

The finished box is a plain two-piece rigid box, and that is the point. Nothing about it is clever. The work was in noticing that the product stops being solid at 28 °C and in refusing to hold it, smell it or stack it — which are the three things a presentation insert is normally designed to do.
