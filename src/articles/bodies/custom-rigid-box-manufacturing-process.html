A rigid box is the structure buyers reach for when the packaging has to carry the brand rather than simply protect the product. It is also the structure most often misdescribed in a brief, because the mental model is wrong: a rigid box is not printed and folded like a carton. It is wrapped.

That single difference decides which stages exist, why corners are the first thing to fail, why a reorder costs less than the first order, and what you should be inspecting at each handover. What follows is the sequence in the order it actually happens, with the decision that is being made at each stage and the thing worth checking before you approve it.

A rigid box is wrapped, not folded

A folding carton starts as a printed sheet. It is die-cut, creased, folded and glued, and the printed surface is the finished surface. The ink sits on the outside of the structure.

A rigid box starts as greyboard — a dense, non-printed board that provides stiffness and nothing else. That board is then wrapped in paper or fabric that has been printed separately. The wrap goes over the board, around the corners and into the interior, where it becomes the visible inside surface of the box.

Three consequences follow immediately, and they explain most of what surprises buyers:

  • Everything is a fit between two things. The board and the wrap are separate components with separate tolerances, so the wrap allowance at a corner is a real engineering dimension, not a detail.
  • Thicker board does not mean a nicer box. It means a bigger box for the same interior. The interior is what your product uses; the exterior is what you pay to ship.
  • The inside is a design surface. Because the wrap lines the inside too, a rigid box can carry print on the interior at almost no extra cost — which is where most of the unboxing effect actually comes from.

Stage one: board selection

Greyboard is specified in millimetres, and the range that matters commercially is roughly 1.5 mm to 3 mm. The choice is driven by three things, in this order of importance.

Product weight. A box carrying 400 g of glass behaves differently from one carrying 40 g of jewellery. Board that is too thin does not fail dramatically; it flexes, and flexing is what eventually cracks a corner and lifts a wrap.

Box footprint. A large panel needs more board than a small one to resist the same load, because a panel's stiffness falls faster than its area grows. A 300 mm box in 1.5 mm board will feel flimsy even with almost nothing in it.

Stack height. Boxes are stacked in a carton, in a pallet, and in a container. The bottom box carries every box above it. E-commerce and retail distribution add a fourth stack. If the box will be palletised, the board has to survive the load rather than just the product.

What to check before moving on: that the board thickness was chosen against your product rather than copied from a previous project. It is the single most common cause of a box that arrives feeling wrong, and it is cheap to change now and expensive to change after the dieline exists.

Stage two: the dieline

The dieline is the drawing that defines every cut and every crease in the wrap. It is the point at which the box stops being a description and becomes a manufactured object, and it is where the corner allowance, the lid clearance and the overlap are set.

Two dimensions in the dieline do more work than the rest:

  • Corner allowance. The extra wrap that lets the paper turn a 90-degree corner without pulling back. Too little and the wrap lifts as the adhesive cures; too much and the wrap bunches into a visible fold. Textured papers and fabrics need more than coated paper.
  • Lid clearance. The difference between the lid's interior and the base's exterior. This is what makes the lid either slide on with a controlled fit or fight the customer. It is a real gap, measured in fractions of a millimetre, and it is not something to adjust later.

What to check: whether the dieline was drawn for your wrap material or for a generic one. A dieline that works in coated paper will not necessarily work in a heavy textured stock, and the corner is where the difference shows first.

Stage three: printing the wrap

The wrap is printed as a flat sheet, before it goes anywhere near a box. This is convenient — it means the print is done on equipment suited to print rather than on equipment suited to box forming — but it creates the problem every rigid box project has to solve: colour on a flat sheet and colour on a wrapped, stretched, glued surface are not the same thing.

Two practical points:

  • Print a little over, not exactly to, the dieline. The wrap has to fold around the corner and turn inside. Artwork that stops exactly at the edge leaves a band of bare board along the fold line.
  • Approving a colour digitally is not approving it. If brand colour is critical, approve a physical proof or approve it on the sample. A screen and a coated wrap under the lights of your office and the lights of the factory are four different colours.

Stage four: wrapping and corner forming

This is the stage that most distinguishes a good rigid box from an adequate one, and it is almost entirely manual. The board is positioned, the wrap is applied, and the corners are formed by hand — turned, pressed and tucked so the paper follows the corner without creasing or lifting.

Because it is a hand operation, consistency is the thing to specify and the thing to inspect. Two boxes from the same run should have the same corner tension, the same wrap position and the same absence of a glueline. If a sample arrives with corners that are perfect, ask how many boxes were made to produce it, and what the inspection criterion is on the line.

What to check: the corners, the corners, and the corners. Turn the box over, look at the underside, and look at the interior corners where the wrap turns inside. Corner separation in the field begins as a corner that was never quite tight in the sample.

Stage five: the insert

The insert is what converts a box into packaging. Its job is not decoration; it is to hold the product in one position through every orientation the parcel will experience, including the ones nobody plans for.

Four materials cover most requirements:

  • Die-cut paperboard. Cheapest, lightest, recyclable in one stream with the box. Right for light products that only need locating rather than cushioning.
  • Moulded pulp. Made from recycled fibre, holds a three-dimensional shape, and reads as a sustainable choice because it is one. Heavier than paperboard.
  • Foam and EVA. Highest cushioning per millimetre and the neatest cutouts. Adds volume, which is added freight cost, and is the hardest to recycle.
  • Wrapped rigid fitments. A greyboard structure wrapped like the box itself. Used when the interior has to match the exterior finish — jewellery, watches, spirits.

The critical dimension is not the cutout; it is the clearance around the cutout. A cavity cut to the exact product diameter will not release the product cleanly at assembly speed, and a cavity cut too loosely lets the product move, which is how leaks and chips happen in transit. The usable answer is usually a few tenths of a millimetre, decided by product weight and surface finish.

Stage six: finishing

Finishing is applied to the wrapped box or to the wrap before it is applied, depending on the technique. Foil stamping, embossing and debossing are pressed into the surface, so they happen after wrapping. Lamination and spot UV are applied to the wrap. Screen print can go either way.

The thing to understand about finishing is that each technique has a surface it will not work on. Foil does not adhere reliably to silicone-coated or heavily embossed stocks. Spot UV needs a coated surface to contrast against. Debossing disappears into a deep texture. If two finishes are specified on the same panel, the second may be compromised by the first, and that interaction is decided at sampling.

What to check: adhesion, not appearance. A foil that looks perfect in the sample but flakes when the box is opened has failed, and the test is to press adhesive tape onto the finished area and pull it off.

Stage seven: assembly

Assembly is where the box meets its insert, its accessories and — in a set — its other components. Ribbons are anchored, magnetic closures are positioned, trays are seated, and the finished item is packed for shipping.

Assembly is where a specification quietly changes. A ribbon that was specified as anchored under the wrap becomes a ribbon glued to the base. A magnet specified at two positions becomes one, because the second was awkward. These changes rarely appear in a revision of the drawing, and they surface only when a customer opens the box.

What to check: that the assembled sample matches the drawing, in the parts you cannot see as well as the parts you can. Ask for a photograph of the interior with the insert removed.

Stage eight: quality control and packing

Pre-shipment inspection runs against the approved sample and the written specification, not against a general impression of quality. In practice that means comparing dimensions to the dieline, board thickness to the specified value, print and colour to the approved reference, finishes to an adhesion and appearance test, assembly to the drawing and count to the order.

The output that matters is not a pass or fail. It is a record of what was measured and what it measured at, so that a dispute two shipments later is decidable rather than a matter of opinion.

What to check: ask what was measured, not whether it passed. A supplier who can send you the measured values is a supplier whose process you can reason about.

What an approved sample actually is

The sample is not a preview. It is the reference document for the production run — the physical definition of what you agreed to buy, in a form that can be put next to a produced box and compared.

That has two consequences worth acting on. First, sign it and date it, and keep a copy. Second, approve it by loading the actual product, closing the lid, turning the box over and carrying it by the base, rather than by looking at it. A sample approved on appearance alone has approved nothing that matters.

The handovers, and what to approve at each

  • Brief to structure: the structure suits the product's weight and how it will be opened.
  • Structure to dieline: the corner allowance matches the actual wrap material.
  • Artwork to print: the artwork bleeds past the fold lines, and colour is approved physically.
  • Wrapping to finishing: the two finishes specified do not compromise each other on this stock.
  • Insert to assembly: the product is held, not merely located, in every orientation.
  • Assembly to production: the assembled sample matches the drawing in the parts you cannot see.
  • Production to shipment: measured values are recorded against the specification.

None of this requires a buyer to become a packaging engineer. It requires knowing which decision is being made at each handover, so that the approval you give is about the right thing — and so that when something does go wrong, you can say where it started rather than only that it did.
